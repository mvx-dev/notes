\section{Topic 1: Periodic Signals and Fourier Series}
\subsection{Waveforms and Periodic Functions}
\subsubsection{Waveform Fundamentals}
\begin{callout}{definition}[Definition]
\label{definition:t1-d1}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A waveform describes the shape of a signal as a function of time.
    \item A signal is a function that varies with time.
    \item A unit step signal stays at 0 until a certain time, then instantly becomes 1.
    \item A ramp signal increases linearly with time, starting from 0.
\end{itemize}
\end{callout}

The period ($T$) is the time taken for a waveform to complete one full cycle and start repeating
\[
    \text{frequency } f=\frac{1}{T}\text{Hertz (Hz)}.
\]
Hertz is cycles per second. The units of period is time.

\begin{callout}{example}[Example]
\label{example:t1-e1}
Some common frequencies.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \label{tab:t1-t1}
    \begin{tabular}{c|c}
        Frequency ($f$) & Period ($f=\frac{1}{T}$) \\
        \hline
        \hline
        1 kHz & $1\times 10^{-3}s=1ms$\\
        1 MHz & $1\times 10^{-6}s=1\mu s$ \\
        1 GHz & $1\times 10^{-9}s=1ns$ \\
        1 THz & $1\times 10^{-12}s=1ps$
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}
\end{callout}

The peak amplitude of a wave is the distance between the $x$ axis and the highest point of the wave. The peak-to-peak amplitude is two times this amplitude.

\begin{callout}{definition}[Periodic Waveforms (or Functions)]
\label{definition:t1-d2}

    A periodic waveform or function is any waveform or function that satisfies
    \[
        x(t)=x(t+T),
    \]
    where $t$ is the time variable, and $T$ is the period. In general,
    \[
        x(t)=x(t+nT),\forall n \in\mathbb{Z}.
    \]
\end{callout}

\subsubsection{Sinusoid waveforms}
A sinusoidal waveform is a type of period waveform, and can be described using the following equation
\[
    x(t)=A\sin(2\pi ft),
\]
where $x(t)$ is the instantaneous amplitude, $A$ is the peak amplitude, and $f$ is the frequency in Hz. It can also be described in terms of period with
\[
    x(t)=A\sin \left( \frac{2\pi }{T}t \right),
\]
where $T$ is the period. This can be simplified in terms of angular frequency $\omega =2\pi f=\frac{2\pi }{T}$ radians/sec as
\[
    x(t)=A\sin(\omega t).
\]

\subsubsection{Other Types of Waveforms}
\textbf{Square Waveform}

A square waveform is used mostly in digital electronic circuits for clock and timing control signals. It has very steep vertical transitions and flat top and bottoms.

It has pulse width $\tau =\frac{T}{2}$ and frequency $f=\frac{1}{T}$.

\noindent\textbf{Rectangular Waveform}

A rectangular waveform is similar to a square wave, however the duration of the high level (pulse width) is shorter than half the period. This is described by the duty cycle, which is the amount of time a signal is ON compared to the total time of one cycle.

\noindent\textbf{Triangular Waveform}

A triangular waveform is a non-sinusoidal waveform that linearly oscillates between a positive and negative peak. They are usually comprised of many sine or cosine waves with different frequencies and amplitudes.

\noindent\textbf{Sawtooth Waveform}

A sawtooth waveform is a periodic waveform whose shape resembles the teeth of a sawblade. It is similar to a triangular waveform, however the decay is more similar to a square wave's.

\subsubsection{Harmonic Components}
A harmonic is a multiple of the fundamental angular frequency of a waveform. The fundamental frequency is the frequency as defined above, giving the harmonics to be
\[
    \omega _0=2\pi f_0=\frac{2\pi }{T}\quad\Rightarrow\quad w_n=nw_0,f_n=nf_0,
\]
where $n$ is a positive integer.

The average value of a waveform is the mean level of the signal over one complete cycle. This is the overall DC component of the waveform. For a periodic waveform $x(t)$ with period $T$, this is
\[
    V_{avg}=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^Tx(t)dt.
\]

\subsection{Fourier Series}
\subsubsection{Frequency Domain Representation}
The frequency domain representation of a waveform shows how a time-domain (i.e. amplitude as a function of time) signal is distributed across different frequencies, effective decomposing a signal into it's component frequencies.

\begin{callout}{example}[Example]
\label{example:t1-e2}
What does the phase-domain of the sinusoid $v(t)$ below look like?
\[
    v(t)=\frac{4}{\pi }\sin(\omega_0t) + \frac{4}{3\pi }\sin(3\omega_0t)+\frac{4}{5\pi }\sin(5\omega_0t).
\]

\textbf{Answer:}
This would be a plot with three peaks; one at $\omega_0$, one at $3\omega _0$, and one at $5\omega_0$. The first one has a height of $4/\pi $, the second $43\pi $, and the last $4/5\pi $.
\end{callout}

\subsubsection{Fourier Series}
This process of breaking down a signal is called Fourier decomposition, resulting in a Fourier series. This is a sum of sinusoidal components with different frequencies and amplitudes.
The main application of a Fourier series is to determine the harmonic components that are present in a complex periodic waveform.

Any periodic waveform with a period of $T$ seconds can be represented by an infinite series of harmonically related sinusoids. The representation is known as the trigonometric Fourier series.
\[
    x(t)=A_0+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} A_n\cos(n\omega _0t)+B_n\sin(n\omega _0t).
\]
The fundamental frequency of $x(t)$ is $\omega _0=2\pi f_0=\frac{2\pi }{T}$ radians/sec. The integer multiples of $\omega_0$ are known as harmonic frequencies of $x(t)$. The Fourier coefficients $A_0$, $A_n$ and $B_n$ are calculated directly from the signal $x(t)$ using the following equations
\begin{align*}
    A_0 &= \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t)dt \\
    \omega _0&=2\pi f_0=\frac{2\pi }{r}\text{ radians/sec} \\
    A_n&=\frac{2}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t)\cos(n\omega _nt)dt\\
    B_n&=\frac{2}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t)\sin(n\omega _nt)dt.
\end{align*}

\noindent\textbf{Fourier Series of an Even Periodic Function}

A function is defined as even if $x(t)=x(-t)$. For these functions/waveforms, the expressions for the Fourier coefficients reduce to
\[
    A_0=\frac{2}{T}\int_0^{T/2}x(t)dt,\quad A_n=\frac{4}{T}\int_0^{T/2}x(t)\cos(n\omega _0t)dt,\quad B_n=0.
\]
The reason that the $B$ coefficients disappear is because the periodic waveform is even, so there are no terms from the odd sine function.

\noindent \textbf{Fourier Series of an Odd Periodic Function}

A function is defined as odd if $x(t)=-x(t)$. For these function/waveforms, the expressions reduce to
\[
    A_0=A_n=0 \text{ for } n>0,\quad B_n=\frac{4}{T}\int_0^{T/2}x(t)\sin(n\omega _0t)dt
\]

\subsubsection{Reconstructing a Waveform}
To reconstruct (or synthesise) a waveform/function from a set of Fourier coefficients, simply plug them back into the above expression for Fourier series along with their index.

\subsection{Alternative Trigonometric Form}
In the frequency domain, the amplitude of each frequency component is determined by $A_n$ and $B_n$, with contributions from both the sine and cosine terms. We would now like to express the signal in an alternative form in which the amplitude of each frequency components is obtained using a single cosine function with a phase angle
\[
    x(t)=K_0=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} K_n\cos(n\omega_0t+\phi _n).
\]
This is the amplitude-phase representation. We are able to find the phase angle $\phi _n$ with
\[
    \phi _n=\tan^{-1}\left( -\frac{B_n}{A_n} \right)=-\tan^{-1}\left( \frac{B_n}{A_n} \right).
\]
With this phase angle, we are also able to express the Fourier series using a complex number with $K_n\angle\phi _n=A_n-jB_n$.

\subsection{Complex Form of the Fourier Series}
The cosine-sine and amplitude-phase Fourier series from above are all trigonometric Fourier series. Another alternative Fourier series representations uses exponential functions instead of trigonometric functions. This form is therefore called the complex form of the (or exponential) Fourier series, and is given by
\[
    x(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} C_ne^{jn\omega _0t},\quad C_n=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^Tx(t)e^{-jn\omega _0t}dt,
\]
where $\omega _0=\frac{2\pi }{T}$. The DC component is then given by $C_0$, and $C_n$ can be found with
\[
    C_n=\frac{1}{2}(A_n-jB_n),\quad |C_{-n}|=|C_n|=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{A_n^2+A_n^2}.
\]
\begin{itemize}
    \item For even symmetry: $C_n=\frac{2}{T}\int_0^{T/2}x(t)\cos(n\omega _0t)dt$
    \item For odd symmetry: $C_n=-\frac{2j}{T}\int_0^{T/2}x(t)\sin(n\omega _0t)dt$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Effect of Shifting}
Shifting a periodic function along either the vertical or the horizontal axis will generally affect its Fourier coefficients. These are also affected by the presence of certain types of functional symmetries in $f(t)$, particularly even-, odd-, and half-wave symmetry because some coefficient sets vanish altogether.

Shifting a periodic function $f(t)$ along either the vertical or the horizontal axis affects its amplitude-phase Fourier series. It is apparent that shifting $f(t)$ up or down the vertical axis changes only the average value $K_0$, leaving the amplitude $K_n$ and phase angle $\phi _n$ of each harmonics component unaffected.

\subsection{Half-Wave Symmetry}
An important type of symmetry is the half-wave symmetry. A periodic function is half-wave symmetric if
\[
    x(t)=-x \left( t-\frac{T}{2} \right).
\]
Shifting a half-wave symmetric wave or function by half a period then inverting it yields the original function. If a function has half-wave symmetric, then both $A_n$ and $B_n$ are 0 for even values of $n$.

\subsubsection{Odd Half-Wave Symmetry}
A periodic signal has odd half-wave symmetry is both of the following are true:
\[
    x(t)=-x \left( t-\frac{T}{2} \right),\quad \text{and}\quad x(t)=-x(-t).
\]

For a function with odd half-wave symmetry, the coefficients are given by
\begin{align*}
    A_n &= \begin{cases}
        \frac{4}{T} \int_0^{T/2}x(t)\cos(n\omega _0t)dt & \text{for n odd} \\
        0 & \text{for n even}
    \end{cases} \\
        B_n&=\begin{cases}
            \frac{4}{T}\int_0^{T/2}x(t)\sin(n\omega _0t)dt & \text{for n odd} \\
            0 & \text{for n even}
        \end{cases}
\end{align*}

For odd half-wave symmetry
\begin{itemize}
    \item All cosine terms are 0 $A_n=0$.
    \item All even harmonics are 0.
    \item Only odd sine harmonics appear in the Fourier series.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Even Half-Wave Symmetry}
A periodic signal has even half-wave symmetry is both of the following are true:
\[
    x(t)=-x \left( t-\frac{T}{2} \right),\quad \text{and}\quad x(t)=x(-t).
\]

For a function with even half-wave symmetry, the coefficients are given by
\begin{align*}
    A_n &= \begin{cases}
        \frac{4}{T} \int_0^{T/2}x(t)\cos(n\omega _0t)dt & \text{for n odd} \\
        0 & \text{for n even}
    \end{cases} \\
        B_n&=\begin{cases}
            \frac{4}{T}\int_0^{T/2}x(t)\sin(n\omega _0t)dt & \text{for n odd} \\
            0 & \text{for n even}
        \end{cases}
\end{align*}

For even half-wave symmetry
\begin{itemize}
    \item All sine terms are 0 $B_n=0$.
    \item All sine harmonics are 0.
    \item Only odd cosine harmonics appear in the Fourier series.
\end{itemize}

\section{Topic 2: Aperiodic Signals and Fourier Transforms}
\subsection{Periodic and Aperiodic Signals}
A periodic signal repeats itself after some period $T$, and can be represented by
\[
    x(t)=x(t+nT),\quad \forall n \in \mathbb{Z}.
\]
The frequency components of a periodic signal are analysed using a Fourier series. An aperiodic signal has no repeating pattern, and we assume it's period is infinite. Most practical signals are aperiodic. Since the fundamental frequency is inversely proportionate to the period, so as the period increases indefinitely the individual spectral components merge into a continuous spectrum and the fundamental frequency becomes vanishingly small.

As a consequence,the frequency $n\omega _0$ of each harmonic component becomes the continuous frequency variable $\omega $, the line spacing $\omega _0$ becomes the infinitesimal $d\omega $, and the operation of summation becomes the operation of integration.

Consider the exponential Fourier series. We have 
\[
    x(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} C_n e^{jn\omega _0t},\quad C_n=\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t)e^{-jn\omega _0t}dt.
\]
This then gives the full expression for $x(t)$ to be
\[
    x(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \left\{ \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t)e^{-j\omega _0t}dt \right\}e^{jn\omega _0t},
\]
and the inverse to be
\[
    f(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \left\{ \frac{1}{2\pi }\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t)e^{-jn\omega _0t}dt \right\}e^{jn\omega _0t}\omega _0.
\]
We can turn this Fourier series into a Fourier transform by taking the limit of $T\to \infty$, replacing the frequency with the continuous frequency, the line spacing with $d\omega $, and integrating instead of summing. This gives the Fourier transform (FT) of $x(t)$ (denote $X(\omega )$ or $X(j\omega )$) to be
\[
    X(\omega )=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j\omega t}dt,
\]
and the inverse Fourier transform (IFT) to be
\[
    x(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi }\int_{-\infty}^\infty X(\omega )e^{j\omega t}d\omega .
\]
Note that $X(\omega )$ is a complex-valued function, and $x(t)$ is normally a real valued signal.

\subsubsection{Fourier Series to Fourier Transform}
\begin{itemize}
    \item For small $T$, the signal repeats frequently in time and its frequency spectrum consists of discrete spectral lines that are widely spread. As $T$ increases and approaches $\infty$, the pulses move further apart in time, the fundamental frequency decreases, and the spacing between spectral lines becomes smaller
    \item As a consequence, more frequency components appear within a given frequency range, and the discrete spectrum begins to resemble a continuous one
    \item In the limit, the periodic signal becomes an aperiodic single pulse, the spectral lines merge into a continuous spectrum, and the Fourier series representation naturally transitions to the Fourier transform.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Fourier Transform Properties}
Let $f(x)$ and $h(x)$ represent integrable functions satisfying
\[
    \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left| f(x) \right|dx <\infty.
\]
We denote the Fourier transforms of these functions as $F(\xi )$ and $H(\xi )$, respectively.

\subsubsection{Basic Properties}
The Fourier transform has the following basic properties
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Linearity:} $af(x)+bh(x) \xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}aF(\xi )+b H(\xi),\quad a,b \in \mathbb{C}$
    \item \textbf{Time shifting:} $f(x-x_0) \xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}} e^{-i 2\pi x_0\xi }F(\xi ),\quad x _0\in \mathbb{R}$.
    \item \textbf{Frequency shifting:} $e^{i2\pi \xi _0x}f(x) \xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}F(\xi -\xi _0),\quad \xi _0\in \mathbb{R}$
    \item \textbf{Time scaling:} $f(ax) \xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{|a|}F \left( \frac{\xi }{a} \right),\quad a\neq 0$. Note: the case $a=-1$ leads to the time-reversal property: $f(-x) \xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}F(-\xi )$
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Differentiation}
Suppose $f(x)$ is differentiable almost everywhere, and both $f$ and its derivative $f'$ are integrable. The Fourier transform is then given by
\[
    F'(\xi )=\mathcal{F}\left\{ \frac{d }{d x}f(x) \right\}=i 2\pi \xi F(\xi ).
\]
We also have differentiation in time to be
\[
    \frac{d }{d t}f(x) \xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}} j\xi F(\xi ),
\]
which is multiplying the frequency by $j\xi $. Similarly, taking the integrate w.r.t time gives
\[
    \int_{-\infty}^tf(x)dx \xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}\frac{F(\xi )}{j\xi }.
\]

And for frequency we have
\[
    -jtf(x) \xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}\frac{d }{d \xi }F(\xi ),
\]
which is effectively multiplication in time by $jt$.

\subsubsection{Convolution}
Convolution describes how the output of a system depends on the input signal and the system's impulse response. Taking the same functions $f(x)$, $g(x)$ from before (along with their transforms), we get an output signal $y(x)$ given by
\[
    y(x)=f(x)*g(x)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(\tau )g(x-\tau )d\tau ,
\]
where $*$ denotes the convolution operation, then
\[
    Y(\xi )=F(\xi )G(\xi ).
\]
Convolution in the time domain corresponds to multiplication in the frequency domain. Note that when the input is an impulse $f(x)=\delta (x)$, then
\[
    Y(\xi )=G(\xi ),
\]
since $X(\xi )=1$.

\subsubsection{Modulation}
Amplitude modulation is the process of varying the amplitude of a sinusoidal carrier. If the modulating signal is denoted $x(t)$, the modulated carrier becomes
\[
    y(t)=x(T)\cos(\omega _0t).
\]
The amplitude spectrum of this carrier is one-half the amplitude spectrum of $x(t)$ shifted to be centred at $\pm\omega _0$
\[
    \mathcal{F}\left\{ x(t)\cos(\omega _0t) \right\}=\frac{1}{2}X(\omega -\omega _0)+\frac{1}{2}X(\omega +\omega _0).
\]

\section{Topic 3: Laplace Transforms of Signals and Circuits}
\subsection{Introduction to Laplace Transform}
A Laplace transform can be thought of as a generalisation of Fourier transforms that transforms differential equations into algebraic equations. It is also able to handle initial conditions, transients, and steady-states responses.

It is an integral transformation that maps a function $f(t)$ from the time domain to the $s$-domain. It is defined as
\[
    \mathcal{L}\left\{ f(t) \right\}=F(s)=\int_0^{\infty}f(t)e^{-st}dt,
\]
where $s$ is a complex variable and is given by $s=\sigma +j\omega $, $\sigma $ is the exponential damping factor, and $\omega $ is the frequency. Note that the Fourier transform is a special case when $\sigma =0$,
\begin{itemize}
    \item $s=j\omega $ - sinusoidal AC signals (Fourier/frequency axis)
    \item $s=\sigma $ - for exponential signals
    \item $s=\sigma +j\omega $ - for exponential sinusoidal signals.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Laplace Transform Properties}
Let $f(t)$ and $g(t)$ be two functions with respective Laplace transforms $F(s)$ and $G(s)$
\[
    f(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ F(s) \right\},\quad g(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ G(s) \right\}.
\]
\subsubsection{Basic Properties}
The Laplace transform has the following basic properties (note: the $u(t-a)$ function is present to ensure the function doesn't include negative time):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Linearity}: $af(t)+bg(t)\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}aF(s)+bG(s)$
    \item \textbf{Time scaling}: $f(at)\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}\frac{1}{a}F \left( \frac{s}{a} \right)$
    \item \textbf{Time shifting}: $f(t-a)u(t-a)\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}e^{-as}F(s)$
    \item \textbf{Frequency shifting}: $e^{-as}f(t)u(t)\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}F(s+a)$
    \item \textbf{First time derivative}: $\frac{d f(t)}{d t}\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}sF(s)-f(^-)$
    \item \textbf{Second time derivative}: $\frac{d^2f(t)}{d t^2}\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}s^2F(s)-sf(0^-)-f'(0^-)$
    \item \textbf{General time derivative}: $\frac{d ^n}{d t^n}f(t)\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}s^nF(s)+\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ -s^{n-k}f^{k-1}(0^-) \right]$
    \item \textbf{Time integration}: $\int_0^tf(\tau )d\tau \xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}\frac{1}{s}F(s)$
    \item \textbf{Differentiation in s-domain}: $tf(t)\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}-\frac{d F(s)}{d s}$
    \item \textbf{Differentiation in s-domain (general)}: $t^nf(t)\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}(-1)^nF^{(n)}(s)$
    \item \textbf{Frequency Integration}: $\frac{f(t)}{t}\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}\int_s^\infty F(s)ds$
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Initial and Final Value Theorem}
The initial and final values of a function can be directly found from its Laplace transform. We have the initial value to be
\[
    f(0)=\lim_{s\to\infty} sF(s),
\]
and final value as
\[
    f(\infty)=\lim_{s\to0} sF(s).
\]
Note that the final value theorem is only applicable when the system has a definite final value. For rational $F(s)$, the poles of $sF(s)$ must lie in the open left-half place (aside from the simple origin case).

\begin{callout}{example}[Example]
\label{example:s4-e1}
Find the final value of the function
\[
    f(t)=e^{-2t}\sin(5t)u(t).
\]

\textbf{Answer}: First, we find the Laplace transform of this function to be
\[
    F(s)=\mathcal{L}\left\{ f(t) \right\}=\frac{5}{(s+2)^2+4s^29}.
\]
Taking the limit gives
\begin{align*}
    f(\infty) &= \lim_{s\to 0} sF(s) \\
              &= \lim_{s\to 0}\frac{5s}{s^2+4s+29} \\
              &= 0.
\end{align*}
\end{callout}

\subsection{Inverse Laplace Transform}
The inverse Laplace transform is very similar to the formula for the inverse Fourier transform. We have
\[
    f(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ F(s) \right\}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{T\to \infty} \int_{\gamma -i T}^{\gamma +i T}e^{st}F(s)ds,
\]
where $\gamma =\mathfrak{Re}(s)$, such that $\gamma $ is greater than the real part of all singularities (discontinuities) of $F$ and $F$ is bounded on the line. In the special case where all singularities lie in the open left half-place, or $F$ is an entire function (such as a polynomial, exponential, finite sum, etc), then $\gamma $ can be set to 0 and the above integral is identical to the inverse Fourier transform.

The typical way to find the inverse Laplace transform is to simply using the properties from above, along with a table of basic/common Laplace transform pairs, to find the inverse. This usually also requires decomposing $F(s)$ into partial fractions

\subsubsection{Partial Fraction Decomposition}
The partial fraction decomposition of a rational fraction of the form
\[
    \frac{f(x)}{g(x)},
\]
where $f(x)$, $g(x)$ are polynomials and $g(x)\neq 0$, is the expression of the rational fractions as
\[
    \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=p(x)+\sum_{k} \frac{f_j(x)}{g_j(x)},
\]
where $p(x)$ is a polynomial, and for each $j$, the denominator $g_j(x)$ is a power of an irreducible polynomial (i.e. not factorisable into polynomials of positive degrees), and the numerator $f_j(x)$ is a polynomial of a smaller degree than the degree of the irreducible polynomial. Note that when doing this by hand, we just consider "square-free" polynomials rather than irreducible polynomials.

There are two methods for finding partial fractions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Residue Method}: "Cover" the factor $(x-p)$, then evaluate the rest at $x=p$. See Example \ref{example:s4-e2} for an example of this.
    \item \textbf{Algebraic Method}: Equate $f(x)$ to a coefficient corresponding to one of the factors multiplied by the rest of $g(x)$. Then, the coefficients can be found by solving for each of them. See Example \ref{example:s4-e2} for an example of this.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Laplace-Domain Circuit Models}
Laplace transform is particularly useful for circuit analysis by converting to the $s$-domain of circuit elements. Circuit elements can be transformed into impedances, which is similar to phasor impedances from ELEC1111.

\subsubsection{Resistor}
For resistors, all values remain the same in both the time domain and the $s$-domain.
\[
    v(t)=Ri(t)\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}V(s)=RI(s).
\]

\subsubsection{Inductors}
In the time-domain, we have voltage to be inductances multiplied by the change in current, and the current to be the total change in voltage divided by the inductance,
\[
    v(t)=L \frac{d i(t)}{d t},\quad i(t)=\frac{1}{L}\int_0^tv(\tau )d\tau +i(0),
\]
where $i(0)$ is the current at $t=0$. Taking the Laplace transform of this, we get
\begin{align*}
    v(t)=L \frac{d i(t)}{d t} &\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}} V(s)= sLI(s)-Li(0) \\
    i(t)=\frac{1}{L} \int_0^tv(\tau )d\tau +i(0) &\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}} I(s)=\frac{V(s)}{sL}+\frac{i(0)}{s}.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Capacitors}
In the time-domain, the voltage is given by the total change in current divided by the capacitance, and the current to be the capacitance multiplied by the change in voltage.
\[
    v(t)=\frac{1}{C}\int_0^ti(\tau )d\tau +v(0),\quad i(t)=C \frac{d v(t)}{d t}.
\]
Taking the Laplace transform of this, we get
\begin{align*}
    v(t)=\frac{1}{C}\int_0^ti(\tau )d\tau +v(0) &\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}V(s)=\frac{I(s)}{sC}+\frac{v(0)}{s}  \\
    i(t)=c \frac{d v(t)}{d t} &\xLeftrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}I(s)=sCV(s)-Cv(0).
\end{align*}

In the above equations, the initial conditions represented a voltage or current source on the circuit, indicating the circuit started with a certain current/voltage. If we assume no initial conditions, we get

\subsection{Transfer Functions}
A transfer function describes how a signal is changed whilst passing through a system. The transfer function $H(s)$ is defined as the ratio of the output response $Y(s)$ to input excitation $X(s)$, assuming all initial conditions are zero
\[
    H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)},\quad Y(s)=H(s)\cdot X(s),
\]
where $H(s)$ is the transfer function, and $X(s)$ and $Y(s)$ are the input and output, respectively. If both $X(s)$ and $Y(s)$ are voltages, then it represents a voltage gain. If both variables are currents, it represents a current gain. This gives four common types of transfer function
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Voltage Gain}: $H(s)=\frac{V_o(s)}{V_i(s)}$
    \item \textbf{Current Gain}: $H(s)=\frac{I_o(s)}{I_i(s)}$
    \item \textbf{Impedance}: $H(s)=\frac{V(s)}{I(s)}$
    \item \textbf{Admittance}: $H(s)=\frac{I(s)}{V(s)}$
\end{itemize}
A single circuit may have several possible transfer function, depending on the chosen input and output variables. Note that gain is dimensionless, whereas impedance and admittance have units.

\subsubsection{Unit Impulse Response}
To determine the transfer function $H(s)$ of a system, both in the input and output functions must be known. If the input $X(s)$ and the transfer function are known, the output is obtained as
\[
    Y(s)=H(s)\cdot X(s).
\]
Taking the inverse Laplace transform,
\[
    y(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ Y(s) \right\}.
\]
If the input is a unit response, $x(t)=\delta (t)$, then
\[
    X(s)=1,
\]
and therefore $Y(s)=H(s)$, which also means
\[
    y(t)=h(t),
\]
where
\[
    h(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ H(s) \right\}.
\]
The function $h(t)$ is called the unit impulse response. It represents the time-domain response of the system to a unit impulse input. Therefore, the transfer function $H(s)$ is the Laplace transform of the unit impulse response of the system.

\begin{callout}{example}[Example]
\label{example:s4-e2}
Find the inverse Laplace transform of
\[
    F(s)=\frac{s^2+12}{s(s+2)(s+3)}
\]
using both the Residue method and the algebraic method for partial fraction decomposition.

\textbf{Answer}: First, we need to find the partial fractions
\[
    \frac{s^2+12}{s(s+2)(s+3)}=\frac{A}{s}+\frac{B}{s+2}+\frac{C}{s+3}.
\]

\textbf{Algebraic Method}:
Multiplying both sides by the denominator, we get
\[
    s^2+12=A(s^2+5s+6)+B(s^2+3s)+C(s^2+2s).
\]
Equating the coefficients of like powers, we get
\begin{align*}
    \text{Constant}&:& 12&=6A& &\Rightarrow A=2 \\
    s&:             & 0&=5A+3B+2C& &\Rightarrow 3B+2C=-10 \\
    s^2&:           & 1&=A+B+C& &\Rightarrow B+C=-1 \\
       & & \therefore A&=2,B=-8,C=7.
\end{align*}

\textbf{Residual Method}:
To find each coefficient, we just multiply the denominator of the respective fraction by $F(s)$. This gives the following set of equations
\begin{align}
    A &= sF(s)\bigg\rvert_{s=0}\label{eq:s4-e2-e1}
 \\
    B&= (s+2)F(s)\bigg\rvert_{s=-2}\label{eq:s4-e2-e2}
 \\
C&= (s+3)F(s)\bigg\rvert_{s=-3}. \label{eq:s4-e2-e3}
\end{align}
Evaluating these gives
\begin{align*}
    \eqref{eq:s4-e2-e1}\quad A &= \frac{s^2+12}{(s+2)(s+3)}\bigg\rvert_{s=0} \\
                               &= \frac{12}{2\times 3} =2\\
    \eqref{eq:s4-e2-e2}\quad B&= \frac{s^2+12}{s(s+3)}\bigg\rvert_{s=-2}\\
                              &= \frac{12}{-2\times 1} = -8 \\
    \eqref{eq:s4-e2-e3}\quad C&= \frac{s^2+12}{s(s+2)} \\
                              &= \frac{12}{-3\times -1} = 7.
\end{align*}
We can see that these give the same result, however the second method was easier. We now have our function to be in the form
\[
    F(s)=\frac{2}{s}-\frac{8}{s+2}+\frac{7}{s+3},
\]
which we can find the inverse of using linearity and a table of common pairs to get
\begin{align*}
    f(t)&=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left( \frac{2}{s} \right)-\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left( \frac{8}{s+2} \right)+\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left( \frac{7}{s+3} \right) \\
        &= 2u(t)-8e^{-2t}+7e^{-3t},\quad t\geq 0
\end{align*}
\end{callout}

\part{System Analysis}
\section{Topic 4: System Analysis}
An analogue system is a device that operates on a continuous time signal $x(t)$, called the input, and produces another continuous time signal $y(t)$, called the output (or response), according to a well-defined rule.

\subsection{$s$-Plane}
Usually, it is best to represent the complex frequency $s=\sigma +j\omega $ graphically in the complex plane, called the $s-plane$. The horizontal axis $\sigma $ represents the real part of $s$, i.e. the exponential damping factor, and the vertical axis $j\omega $ represents the imaginary part of $s$, i.e. the sinusoidal frequency $\omega $.

The Fourier transform can be obtained from the Laplace transform by setting $\sigma =0$. Thus, if the region of convergence for the Laplace transform includes the imaginary axis, then,
\[
    \text{FT}: H(j\omega )=H(s)\rvert_{s=j\omega }.
\]In this plane, $\sigma =0$ corresponds to the imaginary axis. Therefore, the Fourier transform is obtained by evaluating the Laplace transform along the imaginary axis.

\subsection{Poles and Zeros}
\textbf{Poles}: When the denominator $X(s)$ of a rational transfer function $H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}$ of a system is set equal to zero, it is known as the characteristic equation. The roots of this equation determine the character of the time response of the system. These roots are called the poles of the system, and they are where the transfer function becomes infinite.

\noindent\textbf{Zeros}: Similarly, the roots of the numerator polynomial $Y(s)$ of a transfer function are called the zeros of the system, and correspond to where the transfer function is zero.

We denote the location of zeros in the $s$-plane with the symbol $0$, and the location of poles with the symbol $\times$.

\subsection{System Stability}
\subsubsection{Real Pole(s)}
If all of a systems pole lie in the left-half of the $s-plane$, then that system is said to be stable. They correspond to exponentially decaying impulse responses. For a causal continuous-time linear-time-invariant (LTI) system, Bounded-Input Bounded-Output (BIBO) stability requires all poles of $H(s)$ to lie strictly in the open left-half plane, i.e. $\mathfrak{Re}(p)<0$.

\begin{callout}{note}[Note]
\label{note:causality}
A system is causal if its output at time $t$ only depends on present and past inputs, not future inputs. For an LTI system, this means its impulse satisfies
\[
    H(t)=0,\quad \forall t<0.
\]
This means that if a unit impulse response contains $u(t)$, it is inherently causal.
\end{callout}

If the poles instead lie in the right half-plane, then they correspond to systems with exponentially growing impulse responses, and are called unstable as they do not converge.

\subsubsection{Complex Conjugate Poles}
Consider a transfer function with a pair of complex conjugate poles at
\begin{align*}
    H(s) &= \frac{1}{(s-p_1)(s-p_3)} \\
         &= \frac{1}{(s-(\sigma _a+j\omega _a))(s-(\sigma _a-j\omega _a))}
         &= \frac{A}{s-\sigma _a-j\omega _a}+\frac{B}{s-\sigma _a+j\omega _a}.
\end{align*}
By evaluating the constants $A$ and $B$, we obtain
\begin{align*}
    H(s) &= \frac{\frac{1}{2j\omega _a}}{s-\sigma _a-j\omega _a}+\frac{-\frac{1}{2j\omega _a}}{s-\sigma _a+j\omega _a} \\
    \Rightarrow h(t) &= \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ H(s) \right\} \\
                     &= \frac{1}{2j\omega _a}\left[ e^{(\sigma _a+j\omega _a)t}-e^{(\sigma _a-j\omega _a)t} \right] \\
                     &= \frac{1}{\omega _a}\cdot e^{\sigma _at}\left[ \frac{e^{j\omega _at}-e^{-j\omega _at}}{2j} \right] \\
                     &= \frac{1}{\omega _a}e^{\sigma _at}\sin(\omega _at).
\end{align*}
This is a impulse response $h(t)$ of a second-order system with complex conjugate poles. The real part $\sigma _a$ determines the decay rate, while the imaginary part $\omega _a$ determines the oscillation frequency. Thus, the impulse response depends strongly on the pole locations.

If the real part of a pole is kept constant and the imaginary part increases from $\omega _a$ to $\omega _p$, the envelope of the impulse response $h(t)$ remains the same, while the oscillation frequency increases.

If the imaginary part is held constant and the real part becomes more negative (move further into the left-half plane), the oscillation frequency remains unchanged while the decay rate increases. Notice also that if the pole lies on the real axis (i.e. $\omega _a=0$), then there is no oscillation in the impulse response. If the poles lies on the imaginary axis ($\sigma =0$), there is no growth or decay in the impulse response, it is a pure oscillation. If a system has a pole with a positive real part ($\sigma >0$), its impulse response grows exponentially. Such a system is unstable.

A stable and causal system is a system that has all its poles in the left half-plane. A persistent undamped oscillation is not BIBO stable.

\subsection{Analysis of a System Without Zeros}
\subsubsection{First-Order System}
A first-order system without zeros can be described by the transfer function
\[
    H(s)=\frac{a}{s+a},
\]
with the pole at $-a$. If the input is a unit step function, where $X(s)=\frac{1}{s}$, the Laplace transform of the step response is
\[
    Y(s)=X(s)\cdot H(s)=\frac{1}{s}\frac{a}{s+a}=\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{s+a}.
\]
Taking the inverse transform of $Y(s)$, the step response is given by
\[
    y(t)=1-e^{-at}.
\]
We can define a time constant $T$ to be
\[
    T=\frac{1}{a},
\]
which we can plug into this step response
\[
    y(t)\bigg\rvert_{t=\frac{1}{a}}=1-e^{-a \left( \frac{1}{a} \right)}=0.63,
\]
which means $T$ define the amount of time it takes for the step response to rise to $63\%$ of its final value, and has the unit seconds. The reciprocal of the time constant has the units $\frac{1}{\text{sec}}$, or frequency. We can then call $a$ the cutoff frequency.

The time constant is related to the transient response, which is the speed at which the system responds to a step input. Since the pole of the transfer function is at $s=-a$, we can say the pole is located at the negative of the reciprocal of the time constant, and the further the pole from the imaginary axis the faster the transient response.

\subsubsection{Second-Order System}
In comparison to a first-order system, a second-order system exhibits a wide range of responses. The general case for a second-order transfer function is
\[
    H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}=\frac{a}{s^2+bs+a}.
\]
By assigning appropriate values to $a$ and $b$, we can see the different transient responses. The term $a$ is simply a multiplier that can take any value. If the input $X(s)$ is a unit step input $X(s)=\frac{1}{s}$, then the output $Y(s)=X(S)\cdot H(s)=\frac{1}{s}\frac{a}{s^2+bs+a}$, 
\[
    \therefore y(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left[ \frac{1}{s}\frac{a}{s^2+bs+a} \right].
\]
We can rewrite the transfer function as 
\[
    H(s)=\frac{\omega _n^2}{s^2+2\zeta \omega _ns+\omega _n^2},
\]
which we can use to describe different types of systems

\subsection{Cascade and Parallel Structures}
A linear system can be characterised by its transfer function $H(s)$, where its input $X(s)$ and output $Y(s)$. When the transfer function of the overall system $H(s)$ is the product of two transfer functions, then it is called cascade form (can be thought of as in series)
\[
    H(s)=H_1(s)\cdot H_2(s).
\]
When two transfer functions $H_3(s)$ and $H_4(s)$ appear in parallel, then the overall transfer function is given by
\[
    H(s)=H_3(s)+H_4(s).
\]

\subsection{Frequency Response}
The steady-state response $\sigma =0$ of a linear system to a sinusoidal input is obtained from the expression for $H(s)$ by replacing $s$ with $j\omega $. The function $H(j\omega )=H(\omega )$ is called the frequency response function. The modulus of $H(j\omega )$ gives the magnitude of the sinusoidal response $|H(j\omega )|=|H(\omega )$. The angle of $H(j\omega )$ gives the phase of the response $\theta (\omega )$, relative to the input sinusoid.

\subsubsection{Decibels}
The decibel is a logarithmic measurement unit for sound levels, but also is important in electronics and signal processing. It represents ratios such as power, sound pressure, voltage, intensity, etc. using a logarithmic scale
\[
    \text{decibels } dB=20 \log_{10} \frac{v_{out}}{v_{in}}=10 \log_{10}\left( \frac{v_{out}}{v_{in}} \right)^2=10 \log_{10}\frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}}.
\]
A difference of 3 dB in a reading represents a doubling in power, and -3 dB represents half power. Each successive multiple of ten increases the order of magnitude by 10.

\subsection{Low-Pass Filter}
For a low-pass filter or system, we have
\[
    H(s)=\frac{a}{s+a},
\]
with the magnitude
\[
    \left| H(j\omega ) \right|=\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2+\omega^2}}.
\]
The cutoff occurs when
\[
    \left| H(j\omega ) \right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},
\]
which gives
\[
    \omega =a=\omega _c,
\]
where $\omega _c$ is called the cutoff frequency. The total bandwidth of the system is then
\[
    \text{BW}=a.
\]

A first-order low-pass filter has the form
\[
    H(s)=\frac{\omega _c}{s+\omega _c},\quad H(j\omega )=\frac{\omega _c}{j\omega +\omega _c}
\]
The cutoff frequency can also be defined by when the magnitude drops off by 3 dB. For a second-order low-pass filter, this is just two low-pass filters cascaded together. Both first- and second-order low-pass filters have the same cutoff frequency, and each pole contributes approximately -20 dB/decade to the high-frequency asymptotic slope. A first-order filter rolls off at 20 dB/decade beyond the cutoff frequency, and a second-order filter rolls at at 40 dB/decade asymptotically for low-pass systems with two more poles than zeros, meaning it attenuates unwanted frequencies sharper than a first-order system.

\subsection{Second-Order Low Pass Filter}
A second-order low pass filter has the form
\[
    H(s)=\frac{\omega _0^2}{s^2+b_0s+\omega _0^2},
\]
with bandwidth and cutoff frequency being given by
\[
    b_0=\frac{\omega _0}{Q},\quad \omega _0,
\]
where $Q$ is the quality factor. In the case were the real poles are distinct, the cut-off frequency $\omega _1$ is determined by the dominate pole $\alpha _1$.

When the poles are complex conjugates, it becomes a resonant low-pass filter. It has the same form as the above second-order low-pass filters.

\subsection{Band-Pass Filter}
A band-pass filter has two cutoff frequencies $\omega _u$ and $\omega _l$ which identify the pass-band (i.e. the region in which frequencies are minimally attenuated). The cutoff frequencies are defined as the frequencies for which the magnitude of the transfer function is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ times the original magnitude. There are three important parameters in a band-pass filter.

The first important parameter in a band-pass filter is the centre frequency $\omega _o$, which is defined as the frequency for which a circuit's transfer function is purely real. This is also known as the resonant frequency, and can be found as
\[
    \omega _o=\sqrt{\omega _u\omega _l}.
\]
For band-pass filters, the magnitude of the transfer function is maximum at the centre frequency. 

The second parameter is the bandwidth (BW), which is the width of the pass-band
\[
    BW=\omega _u-\omega _l.
\]

The final parameter is the quality factor $Q$, which is the ratio of the centre frequency to the bandwidth
\[
    Q=\frac{\omega _o}{BW}.
\]
This is a measure of the width of the pass-band, independent of its location on the frequency axis. It also describes the shape of the magnitude plot, independent of frequency.

\subsubsection{Second-Order Band-Pass Filter}
A second-order band-pass filter has the form
\[
    H(s)=\frac{b_0s}{s^2+b_0s+\omega _0^2},
\]
with $b_0$ is again the bandwidth $b_0=\frac{\omega _2}{Q}$, and $Q$ is the quality factor. Note the $s$ in the numerator, this is not present in the resonant low-pass filters.

\subsection{Notch and Band-Stop Filters}
A notch filter suppresses a specific frequency (known as the notch frequency), ideally reducing its magnitude to zero. It has the form
\[
    H(s)=\frac{s^2+\omega _z^2}{\omega _z^2},
\]
where $\omega _z$ is the notch frequency. When complex poles are introduced to a notch filter, it becomes a band-stop filter
\[
    H(s)=\frac{s^2+\omega _z^2}{s^2+b_ps+\omega _p^2}
\]
with $b_p$ the bandwidth of the resonant pole, $\omega _p$ the resonant frequency of the pole, $Q_p$ the quality factor of the pole, and $\omega _z$ the notch frequency. When the pole frequency $\omega _p=\omega _z$, we get a standard notch filter, depending on $b_p=\frac{\omega _p}{Q_p}$, the notch will be narrower or wider.

\subsection{High-Pass Filter}
A high-pass filter passes high frequency signals and attenuates low frequency signals
\[
    H(s)=\frac{s}{s+\omega _c},\quad H(j\omega )=\frac{j\omega }{j\omega +\omega _c}.
\]
This also blocks DC components, because $\left| H(j0) \right|=0$. A second-order high-pass filter with real poles has the form
\[
    H(s)=\frac{s^2}{s^2+b_1s+\omega _1^2},
\]
and similarly with complex poles it has the form
\[
    H(s)=\frac{s^2}{s^2+b_2s+\omega _2^2}.
\]

\section{Part 5: Bode Plot}
\subsection{Decibels}
The ratio of any two values of the same quantity can be expressed in decibels. It is often useful to express the magnitudes of transfer functions in decibels. To convert a transfer function magnitude to decibels, we multiply the common logarithm (base 10) of the transfer function magnitude by 20.
\[
    \left| H(j\omega ) \right|_{dB}=20 \log_{10}\left| \frac{V_{out}(j\omega )}{V_{in}(j\omega )} \right|.
\]
Similarly, the ratios of powers are converted to decibels by taking 10 times the logarithm of the ratio
\[
    dB=10 \log_{10}\left| \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}} \right|.
\]
Note that the decibels equivalents are positive for magnitudes greater than unity, whereas the decibel equivalents are negative for magnitudes less than unity. An advantage of decibels is when plotting is that very small and very large magnitudes can be displayed clearly on a single plot. For cascaded systems, the product of the transfer functions becomes sums in decibels.

A decade is a range of frequencies for which the ratio of the highest frequency to the lowest is 10 (i.e. 2-20Hz is one decade, 50-5000Hz is two decades). An octave is a two-to-one change in frequency (i.e. 10-20Hz is one octave, 2-16Hz is three octaves). The number of decades between two frequencies is given by
\[
    \log_{10}\frac{f_2}{f_1}.
\]
Similarly, the number of octaves between two frequencies is
\[
    \log_2 \frac{f_2}{f_1}=\frac{\log_{10}\frac{f_2}{f_1}}{\log_{10}2}.
\]

\subsection{Bode Plot}
A bode plot is abscissa (logarithmic frequency) vs magnitude (in dB) or degrees. 

\part{AC Circuit Analysis}
\section{AC Fundamentals}
In alternating current (AC), the flow of electric charge periodically changes direction, whereas in direct current (DC), the electric charge only ever flows in a single direction. AC is more efficient and economical to transmit over long distances, and has the advantage of being able to step-up or step-down the voltage using a transformer.

\subsection{Characteristics of Alternating Current and Voltage}
For AC power systems, there are two important quantities,
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Power loss}: the power losses $P_L$ in a conductor are a product of the square of the current and resistance of the conductor 
    \[
        P_L=I^2R.
    \]
    When transmitting a fixed power through a power line, if the current is doubled, the power loss will be four times greater.
\item \textbf{Power transmitted}: the power transmitted $P_T$ is equal to the product of the current and the voltage 
\[
    P_T=VI.
\]
The same amount of power can be transmitted with a lower current by increasing the voltage, which leads to significantly more efficient transmission of power.
\end{itemize}
An alternating current or voltage can have different waveforms, but for the most part we will focus on sinusoidal waveforms, given by 
\[
    v(t)=V_m \sin \left( 2\pi ft \right)=V_m \sin \left( \frac{2\pi }{T}t \right),
\]
where $v(t)$ is the instantaneous voltage at time $t$, $V_m$ is the peak voltage, $f$ is the frequency, and $T$ is the period. Frequency is related to period by 
\[
    f=\frac{1}{T} \text{ Hz},
\]
but we can simplify both by expressing in angular velocity 
\[
    \omega =2\pi f=\frac{2\pi }{f}\text{ rad/s},
\]
which we can use to write our sinusoid as 
\[
    v(t)=V_m \sin\omega t.
\]

\subsection{Phase Angle}
A sinusoidal waveform may or may not always start at $t=0$ (i.e. have it's first 0 crossing at $t=0$). A delay or advance in the waveform is known as the phase angle ($\phi $), which is in degrees or radians. We can include this in our sinusoidal waveform as 
\[
    v(t)=V_m \sin \left( \omega t\pm\phi  \right).
\]
Note that an advance looks like the waveform has been shifted to the left, however this is an advance as it reaches it's peak \textit{before} a sinusoid with no phase angle.
\subsubsection{Phase Difference}
Suppose a waveform $A$ is chosen as the reference waveform with the sinusoid given by 
\[
    v(t)=V_m \sin(\omega t).
\]
A waveform $B$ is said to lead the waveform $A$ by 45\textdegree if it has a phase angle of $\phi =+45 \degree$,
\[
    v(t)=V_m \sin \left( \omega t+45\degree \right).
\]
A waveform $C$ is said to lag the waveform $A$ by 60\textdegree if it has a phase angle of $\phi =-60\degree$,
\[
    v(t)=V_m\sin \left( \omega t-60\degree \right).
\]
Note that a sinusoid can also be expressed as a cosine,
\[
    v(t)=V_m\sin \left( \omega t+90\degree \right)=V_m\cos\omega t,\quad v(t)=V_m\cos(\omega t-90\degree)=V_m\sin\omega t.
\]

\subsection{Measurements of AC Quantities}
For a sinusoidal waveform, we can measure
\begin{itemize}
    \item Peak value: the maximum value the sinusoid reaches
    \item Peak-to-peak value: the voltage difference between the minimum and maximum of the sinusoid
    \item Average value: the average value of the sinusoid, usually called the DC component. This is the offset from the horizontal axis.
    \item Root Mean Square (RMS) value: the measure of the heating effect of the sinusoid. It is the practical amount of power in a sinusoid.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Peak Value}
The peak value of a sinusoid is known as the maximum voltage (or current) of the waveform, and occurs at two different positions in a cycle (one positive peak and one negative peak). The magnitude of these peaks are equal, and are $180\degree$ out of phase.

\subsubsection{Peak-to-Peak Value}
The peak-to-peak value of an AC waveform is the total height between opposite peaks, i.e. the difference between the magnitude of the positive peak and the magnitude of the negative peak.

\subsubsection{Average Value}
The average value of a sinusoid is the average of all its instantaneous values over a full cycle, given by 
\[
    V_{avg}=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^Tv(t)dt=\frac{V_m}{T\omega }\left[ \cos \left( \omega t \right) \right]_{\frac{2\pi }{\omega }}^0.
\]
Since the positive and negative alternations are (usually) identical, the average is zero.

\subsubsection{Root Mean Square Value}
The root mean square value (RMS) is a measure of the heating effect of a sine wave. It can be thought of as the equivalent value of DC that will product the same amount of power in a load as a given sine wave. It is given by 
\[
    v_{rms}=\frac{V_m}{\sqrt{2}},\quad i_{rms}=\frac{I_m}{\sqrt{2}}.
\]
This is independent of the phase angle or frequency of sine wave, and depends only on the amplitude of the waveform. The $I_{rms}$ of a periodic waveform $i(t)$ is defined as the current which produces the heat (i.e. power) in a given resistor $R$ at the same average rate as a direct current $I_{dc}$ 
\[
    P_{average}=RI_{rms}^2=RI_{dc}^2.
\]
Consider a periodic current waveform. We can find $I_{rms}$ as
\begin{align*}
    P_{avg} &= \frac{1}{T}\int_0^Tp(t)dt\\
            &= \frac{1}{T}\int_0^Ti^2(t)Rdt\\
            &= \frac{R}{T}\int_0^Ti^2(t)dt=RI^2_{rms}\\
    \therefore I^2_{rms}&=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^Ti^2(t)dt\\
    I_{rms}&= \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^Ti^2(t)dt}.
\end{align*}
Similarly for voltage,
\[
    V_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^Tv^2(t)dt}.
\]
For a waveform comprised of sinusoids of different frequencies, the RMS value is given by the sum of the squaer of the RMS values of each sinusoid. For an AC current $i(t)$ given by 
\[
    i(t)=I_0+\sum_{k}^{n} I_{m,k}\sin(\omega _kt),
\]
we have the RMS value of $i(t)$ to be 
\[
    I^2_{rms}=I_0^2+\sum_{k}^{n} I^2_{rms,k}.
\]

\subsection{Phasor Representation}
A phasor is a line drawn from an origin that has both magnitude and angle. They are used in AC circuits to indicate time varying quantities of current and voltage. They have the form 
\[
    \mathbf{V}=V_m\phase{\phi },
\]
where $\mathbf{V}$ is the phasor, $V_m$ is the magnitude, and $\phi $ is the phase angle. It is a complex number that represents the amplitude and phase angle of a sinusoid. We can represent it in different forms
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rectangular: $\mathbf{V}=V_m\cos\phi +jV_m\sin\phi $
    \item Polar: $\mathbf{V}=V_m\phase{\phi }$
    \item Exponential: $\mathbf{V}=V_me^{j\phi }$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Voltage-Current Relationship for Components}
\subsubsection{Resistor}
Assume that the current through a resistor is given by 
\[
    i(t)=I_m\sin(\omega t)\quad\Rightarrow\mathbf{I}=I_m\phase{0\degree}.
\]
Therefore,
\[
    v(t)=i(t)R=RI_m\sin\omega t,
\]
which gives the phasor representation of the voltage to be 
\[
    \mathbf{V}=RI_m\phase{0\degree}.
\]
The phase angle of both voltage and current waveforms is $0\degree$, and they are said to be in-phase.

\subsubsection{Inductor}
Assume that the current through the inductor is given by 
\[
    i(t)=I_m\sin\omega t\quad\Rightarrow\mathbf{I}=I_m\phase{0\degree}.
\]
We have
\begin{align*}
    v_L(t) &= L \frac{d i(t)}{d t}\\
           &=\omega LI_m\cos\omega t\\
           &=\omega LI_m\sin \left( \omega t+90\degree \right).
\end{align*}
This gives the phasor representation of the voltage to be 
\[
    \mathbf{V}_L=\omega LI_m\phase{90\degree},
\]
indicating that the voltage leads the current by $90\degree$ OR that the current lags the voltage by $90\degree$.

\subsubsection{Capacitor}
Assume that the voltage across the capacitor is given by 
\[
    v_c(t)=V_m\sin\omega t\quad\Rightarrow\mathbf{V}_c=V_m\phase{0\degree}.
\]
We have
\begin{align*}
    i(t) &= C \frac{d v_c(t)}{d t}\\
         &=\omega CV_m\cos\omega t\\
         &=\omega CV_m\sin \left( \omega t+90\degree \right),
\end{align*}
which gives the phasor representation of the current to be 
\[
    \mathbf{I}=\omega CV_m\phase{90\degree}.
\]
Here, the current leads the voltage by $90\degree$ OR the voltage lags the current by $90\degree$. Note the impedance of a circuit element in an AC circuit is the ratio of the voltage phasor to the current phasor.

\subsection{Reactance}
Reactance is the imaginary component of impedance. Since only inductors and capacitors have a complex impedance, they are the only two that have a reactance.

\subsubsection{Inductor}
For an inductor, the impedance is given by 
\[
    Z_L=j\omega L.
\]
This gives the reactance of the inductor to be 
\[
    X_L=\omega L=2\pi fL\Omega,
\]
which we can use to define the impedance to be 
\[
    Z_L=j\omega L=jX_L.
\]
As frequency increases, the inductive reactance also increases. In a DC circuit, an inductor acts as a short circuit, which transitions to an open circuit at high frequencies.

\subsubsection{Capacitor}
The impedance of a capacitor is given by 
\[
    Z_C=\frac{1}{j\omega C},
\]
which gives the reactance 
\[
    X_C=\frac{1}{wC}=\frac{1}{2\pi fC}\Omega .
\]
In terms of reactance, the impedance of a capacitor is 
\[
    Z_C=\frac{1}{j\omega C}=-jX_c.
\]
As frequency increases, the reactance decreases. In a purely DC circuit, it acts as an open circuit, and as a short circuit at high frequencies.

\subsection{Impedance}
\subsubsection{RC Circuit}
Impedance in a RC (resistor and capacitor in series) is composed of resistance and reactance. The impedance is given by
\begin{align*}
    Z &= R+\frac{1}{j\omega C}\\
      &=R-\frac{j}{\omega C}=R-jX_C.
\end{align*}
We can represent this in phasor form by finding the magnitude,
\begin{align*}
    \left| Z \right| &= \sqrt{R^2+\frac{-1}{\omega C}^2}\\
                    &=\sqrt{R^2+X_C^2},
\end{align*}
and the angle,
\begin{align*}
    \theta  &= \tan^{-1}\left( \frac{-1}{\omega CR} \right)\\
            &=\tan^{-1}\left( -\frac{X_C}{R} \right).
\end{align*}
Here, we take the current to be the reference, however for a parallel RC circuit, the voltage is taken as the reference.

In parallel, we can write the following equations
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{I}_T &= \mathbf{I}_R+j\mathbf{I}_C\\
    \left| I_T \right|&=\sqrt{I_R^2+I_C^2}\\
    \tan\theta &=\frac{\mathbf{I}_C}{\mathbf{I}_R}\\
    \mathbf{I}_T&=\sqrt{I_R^2+I_C^2}\ \phase{0\degree}.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{RL Circuit}
Impedance in an RL (resistor and inductor in series) circuit is composed of resistance and reactance. The impedance is given by
\begin{align*}
    Z &= R+j\omega L\\
      &=R+jX_L,
\end{align*}
which can be turned into a phasor
\begin{align*}
    \left| Z \right| &= \sqrt{R^2+\left( \omega L \right)^2} \\
                     &= \sqrt{R^2X_L^2}\\
    \theta &= \tan^{-1}\left( \frac{\omega L}{R}\right)\\
                     &=\tan^{-1}\left( \frac{X_L}{R} \right).
\end{align*}
If the RL circuit was instead in parallel, we can write the following equations
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{I}_T &= \mathbf{I}_R+\mathbf{I}_L\\
    \left| I_T \right|&=\sqrt{I_R^2+I_L^2}\\
    \tan\theta &=\frac{\mathbf{I}_L}{\mathbf{I}_R}\\
    \mathbf{I}_T&=\sqrt{I_R^2+I_L^2}\ \phase{0\degree}.
\end{align*}

\subsection{Current and Voltage Division}
Current division occurs when two components are connected in parallel. Given two components with impedances $Z_1$ and $Z_2$, the current through each is given by
\begin{align*}
    I_1 &= \frac{Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}I\\
    I_2&=\frac{Z_1}{Z_1+Z_2}.
\end{align*}
If the components were instead in series, they would divide the voltage, giving
\begin{align*}
    V_1 &= \frac{Z_1}{Z_1+Z_2}V\\
    V_2&= \frac{Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}V.
\end{align*}

\]

\subsection{Delta-Wye Transformation}
A Delta $\Delta $ network can be converted to a Wye $\mathrm{Y}$ (or Star network), and vice-versa. This is normally used in three-phase AC circuits. To transform Delta to Wye, each impedance in the Wye network is the product of the impedance in the two adjacent Delta branches, divided by the sum of the three Delta impedances. To transform back, each impedance in the Delta network is the sum of all possible products of Wye impedances taken two at a time, divided by the opposite Wye impedance.

        \draw(a) to[generic, l_=$Z_c$, -] (b);

    \caption{A Delta-Wye ($\Delta -Y$) network.}
    \label{fig:s7-delta-wye}
\end{figure}

From the figure, we can construct the relationship between Delta and Wye. Starting with transforming Delta to Wye,
\begin{align*}
    Z_1 &= \frac{Z_bZ_c}{Z_a+Z_b+Z_c}\\
    Z_2 &= \frac{Z_cZ_a}{Z_a+Z_b+Z_c}\\
    Z_3 &= \frac{Z_aZ_b}{Z_a+Z_b+Z_c}.
\end{align*}
And to transform back,
\begin{align*}
    Z_a &= \frac{Z_1Z_2+Z_2Z_3+Z_3Z_1}{Z_1}\\
    Z_b &= \frac{Z_1Z_2+Z_2Z_3+Z_3Z_1}{Z_2}\\
    Z_c &= \frac{Z_1Z_2+Z_2Z_3+Z_3Z_1}{Z_3}.
\end{align*}

A Delta or Wye circuit is balanced if it has equal impedances (load) in all three branches, i.e.
\begin{align*}
    Z_Y &= Z_1=Z_2=Z_3\\
        &= \frac{1}{3}Z_\Delta,\\
    Z_\Delta &= Z_a=Z_b=Z_c\\
             &= 3Z_Y.
\end{align*}

Since all that matters is the connection between the resistors, different arrangements of nodes and wires can still be a Delta/Wye circuit. For a Delta network, it is equivalent to a Pi network, where one resistor is at the top, and either side the other two resistors extend vertically down. Similarly, a T-network is equivalent to a Wye network, with a single resistor connect between two horizontal resistors extending vertically down. These networks are commonly used in power transmission line models.

\subsection{Voltage and Current Sources}
Sources can be either dependant or independent. A dependent source is controlled by another voltage or current elsewhere in the circuit, for example it might supply 0.5 times the current through a resistor.

\subsubsection{Ideal Sources}
An ideal voltage source has no internal resistance, and it cannot exist in nature. Practically, they always have a small amount of internal resistance, however this is usually less than $1\Omega $. This internal resistance appears in series with an ideal voltage source.

An ideal current source has an infinite internal resistance, and it also cannot exist in nature. Similar to a voltage source, practical sources have a very high (but not infinite) internal resistance, on the order of mega Ohms. This internal resistance is always in parallel with an ideal current source. In the real world, current sources are rarely created using a battery and resistor in series, rather they are typically constructed using transistors.

\subsection{Superposition}
Superposition finding the total current or voltage in a circuit by considering only one source at a time. The general process is
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Retain one independent source at a time in the circuit and replace all other sources with their internal impedances, i.e. a voltage source should be replaced with a short circuit, a current source should be replaced with an open circuit.
    \item Determine the output (current or voltage) due to the single source acting alone using any circuit analysis methods.
    \item Repeat the previous two steps for each of the other independent sources.
    \item Find the total contribution by adding algebraically all the contributions due to the independent sources.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Transformation Between Voltage and Current Sources}
Source transformation allows us to consider a voltage source in series with an impedance the same as a current source in parallel with an impedance, and vice-versa. The impedance in both situations remains the same, however the voltage and current is given by Ohm's law 
\[
    V_S=Z_SI_S \iff I_S=\frac{V_S}{Z_S}.
\]
This can also be used for Thevenin's and Norton's equivalent circuits, which is the same as the DC case.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Remove all the independent sources
    \item Assume a voltage or current source connected across the $a-b$ terminals, generally assumed to be $1\phase{0\degree}$
    \item Calculate the voltage or current, and hence $Z_{Th}=\frac{v_o}{i_o}$
\end{itemize}

\section{AC Circuit Analysis}
\subsection{Power Transfer}
Applying an AC signal to a circuit containing resistive and reactive elements results in power transfer from the source to the circuit. Consider the case that the load is purely resistive. This gives our voltage and current to be 
\[
    v(t)=V_m\cos\omega t,\quad i(t)=I_m\cos\omega t.
\]
Multiplying gives the instantaneous power for the circuit as 
\begin{align*}
    p(t)&=v(t)i(t)\\
        &=V_mI_m \cos^2\omega t,
\end{align*}
which is always positive, and so the energy flows continually in the direction from the source to the load. Now, consider the case where the load is purely inductive. Our voltage and current is now 
\[
    v(t)=V_m\cos\omega t,\quad i(t)=\frac{v(t)}{Z}=I_m\sin\omega t.
\]
This gives the instantaneous power to be 
\begin{align*}
    p(t)&=v(t)i(t)\\
        &=V_mI_m\cos\omega t\sin\omega t\\
        &=\frac{V_mI_m}{2}\sin^2\omega t,
\end{align*}
which gives the average power to be zero. Half the time the power is positive, where the inductor is storing the energy in its magnetic field, and the other half of the time it is returning that energy to the source. That means that in this case, the reactive power flows from the source to the load. Finally, considering a purely capacitive load, we have 
\[
    v(t)=V_m\cos\omega t,\quad i(t)=\frac{v(t)}{Z}=-I_m\sin\omega t.
\]
The instantaneous power is
\begin{align*}
    p(t) &= v(t)i(t)\\
         &=-V_mI_m\cos\omega t\sin\omega t\\
         &=-\frac{V_mI_m}{2}\sin^2\omega t.
\end{align*}
The power of the capacitive load is the same as the inductive load, except with the opposite sign. For this, we say that the reactive power flows from the load, and that reactive power is positive for an inductance and negative for a capacitance.

\subsection{AC Power}
If the load contains both inductance and capacitance with reactive powers of equal magnitude, the reactive powers cancel. The power flows back and forth to inductances and capacitances is called reactive power. Even though no average power is consumed by a pure energy-storage element, reactive power is still important. It is possible to have loads composed of energy-storage elements that draw large currents that require heavy-duty wiring, even though little average power is consumed. This means that electric power delivery companies charge their (industrial) customers for reactive power (at a lower rate) as well as for the total energy delivered.

\subsubsection{Power Calculation}
Consider the voltage, current, and power for a general RLC load, for which the phase $\theta $ can be any value from $-90\degree$ to $+90\degree$. The voltage and current are therefore given by 
\[
    v(t)=V_m\cos\omega t,\quad i(t)I_m\cos \left( \omega t-\theta  \right).
\]
The instantaneous power is then
\begin{align*}
    p(t) &= v(t)i(t)\\
         &=V_mI_m\cos\omega t\cos \left( \omega t-\theta  \right)\\
         &=V_mI_m\cos\omega t \left( \cos\omega t\cos\theta  +\sin\omega t\sin\theta \right)\\
         &=V_mI_m\cos\theta \cos^2\omega t+V_mI_m\sin\theta \sin\omega t\cos\omega t\\
         &=V_mI_m\cos\theta \left[ \frac{1+\cos 2\omega t}{2} \right]+\frac{V_mI_m}{2}\sin\theta \sin 2\omega t\\
         &= \frac{V_mI_m}{2}\cos\theta \left[ 1+\cos 2\omega t \right]+\frac{V_mI_m}{2}\sin\theta \sin 2\omega t.
\end{align*}
Notice that the term involving $\cos 2\omega t$ and $\sin \omega t$ have average values of zero, thus giving the average power to be 
\[
    P=\frac{V_mI_m}{2}\cos\theta =\frac{V_m}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{I_m}{\sqrt{2}}\cos\theta =V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\theta .
\]
The term $\cos\theta $ is called the power factor ($pf$) 
\[
    pf=\cos\theta ,
\]
and $\theta $ is sometimes called the power angle. It is common to state that the current leads (in capacitive loads, i.e. leading power factor) or lags (in inductive loads, i.e. lagging power factor) the voltage. A typical power factor would be stated to be $90\%$ lagging, which means that $\cos\theta =0.9$, and the current lags the voltage.

\subsubsection{Power Factor}
Assuming voltage $v(t)$ as the reference, look at the current $i(t)$ relative to the voltage. If the current is lagging the voltage, i.e. lagging power factor, this implies an inductive load. Inversely, if the current is leading the voltage, i.e. leading power factor, this implies a capacitive load. Note that we assume a voltage as having zero phase. In general, the phase of the voltage may have a value other than zero. Then, $\theta $ should be taken as the phase of the voltage $\theta _v$ minus the phase of the current $\theta _i$ 
\[
    \theta =\theta _v-\theta _i.
\]
The power factor is the cosine of the angle at which the current lags the voltage.

\subsubsection{Reactive Power}
The power flowing back and forth between the source and the energy-storage element is the last term in the power expression above 
\[
    p(t)=\frac{V_mI_m}{2}\sin\theta \sin 2\omega t.
\]
The peak value is defined to be the reactive power $Q$
\[
    Q=\frac{V_mI_m}{2}\sin\theta =V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\theta \text{ VAR}.
\]
Note that this is in units of Voltage-Amperes Reactive, which is the same as Watts except that it is specified to be reactive power. For a purely resistive load,
\[
    \theta =0,\quad \therefore Q=0.
\]
$Q$ does not represent the flow of net energy.

\subsubsection{Apparent Power}
The apparent power is define as the product of the effective voltage $V_{rms}$ and effective current $I_{rms}$, and has units Volt-Ampere (VA). In terms of average power $P$ and reactive power $Q$, it is
\begin{align*}
    P^2+Q^2 &= (V_{rms}I_{rms})^2 \left( \cos^2\theta +\sin^2\theta  \right)\\
            &= \left( V_{rms}I_{rms} \right)^2.
\end{align*}
If we say that we have a 5 kW load, that means that $P=5$ kW. If we have a 5kVA load, $V_{rms}I_{rms}=5$kVA. If we say that a load absorbs 5 kVAR, then $Q=5$ kVAR.

\subsubsection{Power Triangle}
The relationship between real power (average power), reactive power, apparent power, and the power angle can be represented by the power triangle. For an inductive load, in which case $\theta >0$, so the reactive power $Q$ is positive. When $\theta <0$, the reactive power $Q$ is negative.

\subsubsection{Additional Relationships}
Consider a circuit given by a voltage source and a load $\left| Z \right|\ \phase{\theta }$. We have the following relationships
\begin{align*}
    I &= \frac{V}{Z}=\frac{V_m\phase{0\degree}}{\left| z \right|\phase{\theta }}=I_m\phase{-\theta } \\
    \cos\theta &=\frac{R}{\left| z \right|}\\
    \sin\theta &=\frac{X}{\left| z \right|} \\
    I_m&=\frac{V_m}{\left| z \right|}.
\end{align*}
That means we can express the real power in a couple of different ways 
\[
    P=\frac{V_mI_m}{2}\cos\theta =\frac{V_mI_m}{2}\frac{R}{\left| z \right|}=\frac{I_m^2R}{2}=I_{rms}^2R.
\]
Similarly,
\[
    Q=I^2_{rms}X.
\]
For an inductive load $X$ is positive, whereas for a capacitive load $X$ is negative. We can also see that 
\[
    P=\frac{V_{Rrms}^2}{R}\quad \text{and}\quad Q=\frac{V_{Xrms}^2}{X}.
\]

\subsubsection{Complex Power}
Consider a circuit with a voltage source and some impedance in series. The complex power, denoted $S$, delivered to the circuit is given by 
\[
    S=\frac{1}{2}V\bar{I}=\frac{1}{2}\left( V_m\phase{\theta _v} \right)\left( I_m\phase{-\theta _i} \right)=\frac{V_mI_m}{2}\phase{\theta _v-\theta _i}=\frac{V_mI_m}{2}\phase{\theta}.
\]
We can convert this to Cartesian form to get 
\[
    S=\frac{1}{2}V\bar{I}=\frac{V_mI_m}{2}\cos\theta +j \frac{V_mI_m}{2}\sin\theta =P+jQ.
\]
This means that once we know the complex power $S$, we can find the average power, reactive power, and apparent power
\begin{align*}
    P &= \mathfrak{Re}(S)=\mathfrak{Re}\left( \frac{1}{2}V\bar{I} \right)\\
    Q&=\mathfrak{Im}(S)=\mathfrak{Im}\left( \frac{1}{2}V\bar{I} \right)\\
    \text{Apparent Power}&=\left| S \right|=\left| \frac{1}{2}V\bar{I} \right|.
\end{align*}

\subsection{Maximum Power Transfer}
For a resistive circuit, the power transfer is maximised when the load is made equal to the source resistance. Consider a circuit with voltage $V_{oc}$, equivalent impedance $Z_{eq}=R_{eq}+jX_{eq}$, and load impedance $Z_L=R_L+jX_L$. This gives the maximum current to be 
\[
    I_m=\frac{\left| V_{oc} \right|}{\left| Z_{eq}+Z_L \right|}.
\]
To find the maximum power transfer, we need to find suitable conditions for the load parameters $R_L$ and $X_L$
\[
    P_L=R_LI^2_{rms},\quad P_L=R_L \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right)^2 \frac{\left| V_{oc}^2 \right|}{\left( R_{eq}+R_L \right)^2+\left( X_{eq}+X_L \right)^2}.
\]
On average, reactances absorb zero power, and thus any non-zero values of $\left( X_{eq}+X_L \right)^2$ would only reduce $P_L$. We can eliminate this term by imposing $X_L=-X_{eq}$, which gives $P_L$ to become 
\[
    P_L=\frac{1}{2}\left| V_{oc} \right|^2 \frac{R_L}{\left( R_{eq}+R_L \right)^2}.
\]
Since $P_L$ is minimised when $\frac{d P_L}{d R_L}=0,$ then $R_L=R_{eq}$, so for maximum power transfer,
\[
    R_L+jX_L=R_{eq}-jX_{eq}\Rightarrow Z_L=Z_{eq}.
\]
We also have the maximum average power to be 
\[
    P_{L(max)}=\frac{1}{2}\left| V_{oc} \right|^2 \frac{R_{eq}}{\left( R_{eq}+R_{eq} \right)^2},\quad P_{L(max)}=\frac{1}{8}\frac{\left| V_{oc} \right|^2}{R_{eq}}.
\]

\subsubsection{Power Factor Correction}
Large currents can flow through energy storage devices (because of inductance and capacitance, as mentioned above) without average power being delivered. So, in heavy industry, many loads are partially inductive and there is a large amount of reactive power flow, which causes higher currents in the power distribution system. As a result, the lines and transformers must have a higher rating than would be necessary with a purely resistance load. This means that companies charge based on how many energy is delivered at low power factors. This means, it is better to have loads near unity power factor, which is done by placing capacitors in parallel with inductive loads to increase the power factor.

\section{AC Resonance}
Resonance is the tendency of a system to oscillate at maximum amplitude at a certain frequency, known as the resonant frequency. This behaviour exists for anything that is periodic, and can be electrical, mechanical, hydraulic, acoustic, etc. Electrical resonance occurs in an electric circuit at a particular frequency when the imaginary parts of impedances of circuit elements cancel each other, i.e. the total impedance is 0, and when the input voltage and current are in phase.

\subsection{Series Resonance}
In a series RLC circuit, the energy flows back and forth between the capacitance and inductance, and is damped by the resistor.

Consider a series RLC circuit. The impedance seen by the source is
\begin{align*}
    Z &= R+j\omega L+\frac{1}{j\omega C}\\
      &= R+j \left( \omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C} \right)\\
      &= R+jX,\quad \text{where }X=\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}.
\end{align*}
The $X$ component is the net reactance of the circuit. Depending on the applied frequency of the source $\omega $, there are three conditions the circuit can be in
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\omega L< \frac{1}{\omega C}$, i.e. $X=0$. This is capacitive behaviour
    \item $\omega L > \frac{1}{\omega C}$, i.e. $X>0$. This is inductive behaviour
    \item $\omega L=\frac{1}{\omega C}$, i.e. $X=0$. This is a purely resistive circuit with $I$ and $V$ in phase with each other.
\end{enumerate}
This last condition is known as the unity power-factor resonance, and occurs at a specific frequency $\omega _0$ that makes $\omega _0L=\frac{1}{\omega _0C}$,
\[
    \omega _0^2=\frac{1}{LC},\quad \omega =\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}.
\]
This is the resonant frequency of the circuit, and in terms of Hertz it is 
\[
    f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{LC}}Hz.
\]
Even though the individual frequencies are not zero, at resonance frequency they cancel each other out, making the total impedance 0. At resonance, we have
\begin{align*}
    I_0 &=\frac{V_m}{R} \\
    \Rightarrow V_{L 0}&= \left( \omega _0L \right)L_0=\omega _0L \frac{V_m}{R}=\frac{\omega _0L}{R}V_m\\
    V_{C 0}&= \left( \frac{1}{\omega _0C} \right)I_0=\frac{1}{\omega _0C}\frac{V_m}{R}=\frac{1}{\omega _0CR}V_m.
\end{align*}
At series resonance, $V_{L 0}=V_{C 0}$, due to equal impedance, which means 
\[
    \frac{\omega _0L}{R}=\frac{1}{\omega _0RC}.
\]
By definition, the quality factor of the series RLC circuit is given by 
\[
    Q=\frac{\omega _0L}{R}=\frac{1}{\omega _0RC},
\]
which we can rearrange to get 
\[
    Q=\frac{\omega _0L}{R}=\frac{1}{R\sqrt{C/L}}.
\]
At resonance, $\omega =\omega _0$, the voltage across the inductor is given by 
\[
    V_{L 0}=\frac{\omega _0L}{R}V_m=QV_m.
\]
Similar, at resonance the voltage across the capacitor is given by 
\[
    V_{C 0}=\frac{1}{\omega _0RC}V_m=QV_m.
\]
The peak amplitude across the reactive components exceeds the peak amplitude of the applied signal.

\subsubsection{Resonance Voltage Rise}
We can express the voltage and current in terms of phasors to see clearly what is happening.
\begin{align*}
    I &= \frac{V_m}{R}\phase{0\degree}\\
    V_{R 0}&=V_m\phase{0\degree} \\
    V_{L 0}&=QV_m\phase{90\degree}\\
    V_{C 0}&=QV_m\phase{-90\degree}.
\end{align*}
From this, we can see that if $Q>1$, then the peak voltage across the components is greater than the applied signal, which is known as resonance voltage rise. The amount of magnification depends on the quality factor. It is interesting to note that the smaller the resistance $R$ in a series RLC circuit, the higher the value of $Q$, and hence greater amount of voltage rise at resonance. Taking the limit of this, we see
\begin{align*}
    \lim_{R\to 0} Q &= \lim_{R\to 0} \frac{\omega _0L}{R}=\lim_{R\to 0}\frac{1}{\omega CR} \\
                    &= \infty,
\end{align*}
which would result in an infinite voltage rise. This quality factor is also a measure of the frequency selectivity, with a higher $Q$ corresponding to high selectivity, or lower bandwidth.

Bandwidth is the width of the frequency response of a circuit taken when the magnitude is $-3dB$. Bandwidth is given by 
\[
    BW=\omega _U-\omega _L,
\]
i.e. the difference between the highest frequency with magnitude greater than $-3dB$ and the lowest frequency with magnitude greater than $-3dB$. We can then express the quality factor in terms of this bandwidth as 
\[
    Q=\frac{\omega _0}{BW}=\frac{R}{L}.
\]
We can also rewrite the impedance of the circuit in terms of the quality factor and the resonant frequency
\begin{align*}
    Z &= R+j \left( \omega L -\frac{1}{\omega C}\right) \\
      &=R \left[ 1+j \frac{\omega L}{R}-j \frac{1}{\omega CR}\right] \\
      &=R \left[ 1+jQ \left( \frac{\omega }{\omega _0}-\frac{\omega _0}{\omega } \right) \right].
\end{align*}
We can do the same with the magnitude of the current
\begin{align*}
    I_m &= \frac{V_m\phase{0\degree}}{Z}\\
        &=\frac{V_m}{R}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+Q^2 \left( \frac{\omega }{\omega _0}-\frac{\omega _0}{\omega } \right)^2}}.
\end{align*}
The plot of $I_m$ vs $\omega $ for a given $V_m$ has the same profile as the band-pass function. At resonance, the average power $P$ dissipated in the resistance is also maximised, giving 
\[
    P_{\text{max}}=\left( \frac{V_m}{\sqrt{2}} \right)^2 \frac{1}{R}=\frac{V_m^2}{2R}.
\]
The frequencies at which $P$ is down to half its maximum value are the half-power frequency from the bandwidth expression, $\omega _L$ and $\omega _U$. If $Q \gg 1$, then the half-power frequencies are given by the approximate expressions
\begin{align*}
    f_U &\approx f_0+\frac{BW}{2}\\
    f_L&\approx f_0-\frac{BW}{2}.
\end{align*}

\subsection{Parallel Resonance}
In a parallel resonance circuit, we find everything in terms of admittance instead of impedance. We have
\begin{align*}
    Y &= Y_R+Y_C+Y_L\\
      &= G+j\omega C+\frac{1}{j\omega L}\\
      &= G+j \left( \omega C-\frac{1}{\omega L} \right).
\end{align*}
We expect a resonance behaviour when the susceptance $\omega C$ and $-\frac{1}{\omega L}$ cancel each other out, which (as before) is when $\omega =\omega _0$. Hence, we have 
\[
    \omega _0C=\frac{1}{\omega _0L},
\]
giving the resonance frequency to be 
\[
    \omega _0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}.
\]
At resonance, the output voltage has the same phase as the input voltage, and it is purely resistive. The magnitude of the current going through the capacitor and inductor going through the inductor is given by
\begin{align*}
    \left| I_C \right| &= \frac{V_m}{\left( \omega _0C \right)^{-1}}=V_m\omega _0C=I_m \left( R\omega _0C \right),\\
    \left| I_L \right|&= \frac{V_m}{\omega _0L}=\frac{I_mR}{\omega _0L}=I_m \left(  \frac{R}{\omega _0L} \right),
\end{align*}
which are equal at resonance. This gives 
\[
    \left| I_L \right|=\left| I_C \right|\Rightarrow R\omega _0C=\frac{R}{\omega _0L},
\]
which gives the quality factor to be 
\[
    Q=R\omega _0C=\frac{R}{\omega _0L}.
\]
Similar to the series RLC, we can write the admittance in terms of the resonant frequency and quality factor. We can rearrange the admittance as
\begin{align*}
    Y &= G+j \left( \omega C-\frac{1}{\omega L} \right)\\
      &=G \left[ 1+j \left( \omega CR-\frac{R}{\omega L} \right) \right].
\end{align*}
Rewriting, we get
\begin{align*}
    Y &= G \left[ 1+jQ \left( \frac{\omega }{\omega _0}-\frac{\omega _0}{\omega } \right) \right]\\
    V&=V_m\phase{0\degree}, \text{ and } I=I_m\phase{0\degree}\\
    V&=ZI=\frac{1}{Y}I=\frac{I_m\phase{0\degree}}{G \left[ 1+jQ \left( \frac{\omega }{\omega _0}-\frac{\omega _0}{\omega } \right) \right]}\\
     &= RI_m \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+Q^2 \left( \frac{\omega }{\omega _0}-\frac{\omega _0}{\omega } \right)^2}}.
\end{align*}
Even though the individual admittance $Y_L$ and $Y_C$ are non-zero, their sum vanishes at resonance, making the parallel combination of $L$ and $C$ effectively behave as an open circuit. Therefore, the average power dissipated in the resistance is 
\[
    P_{\text{max}}=\left( \frac{I_m}{\sqrt{2}} \right)^2=RI_{rms}^2.
\]
The frequencies at which $P$ is down to half its maximum value are the half power frequencies $\omega _L$ and $\omega _U$, and the half-power $BW=\omega _U-\omega _L$ such that 
\[
    BW=\frac{1}{RC}.
\]

\subsection{Practical Resonant Circuit}
The parallel RLC configuration is known as a tuned circuit, such as in tuned oscillators and tuned amplifiers in radio communications. Of the three components making up an RLC circuit, the inductor is the least ideal because of the winding resistance $R_S$.

We can write a parallel non-ideal RLC circuit as 
\[
    Y=\frac{1}{R}+j\omega C+\frac{1}{R_S+j\omega L},
\]
which gives 
\[
    Y=\frac{1}{R}+\frac{R_S}{R_S^2+\left( \omega L \right)^2}+j\omega \left( C-\frac{L}{R_S^2+\left( \omega L \right)^2} \right).
\]
We set the imaginary part to find the frequency at which $Y$ is purely real,
\begin{align*}
    C-\frac{L}{R_S^2+\left( \omega L \right)^2} &= 0\\
    \Rightarrow \omega _0&=\sqrt{\frac{1}{LC}-\frac{R_S^2}{L^2}}.
\end{align*}
As the inductor approaches being ideal (i.e. the limit $R_S\to 0$), we approach the same resonant frequency from before. With the introduction of this additional resistance, we can also describe the resonant frequency in terms of the quality factor of the coil 
\[
    Q_{coil}=\frac{\omega _0L}{R_S},\quad\Rightarrow\omega _0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}\left[ \sqrt{1-\frac{1}{Q_{coil}^2}} \right].
\]
A parallel circuit has high impedance at its resonant frequency, and therefore it attenuates signals at all frequencies except the resonant frequency. Varying the capacitance of the tuned circuit changes the resonant frequency, and generally inductance is kept constant.

\subsection{Series to Parallel Conversion}
It is possible to select a parallel resistor $R_P$ and inductor $L_P$ such that, at a given frequency $\omega $, the admittance of the parallel circuit equals that of the series circuit 
\[
    \frac{1}{R_P}+\frac{1}{j\omega L_P}=\frac{1}{R_S+j\omega L_S}.
\]
Rationalising both sides and equating the real and imaginary components yields
\begin{align*}
    \frac{1}{R_P}-j \frac{1}{\omega L_P} &= \frac{R_S}{R_S^2+\omega^2L_S^2}-j \frac{\omega L_S}{R_S^2+\omega^2L_S^2}\\
    \therefore \frac{1}{R_P}&=\frac{R_S}{R_S^2+\omega^2L_S^2},\quad \Rightarrow R_P=\frac{R_S^2+\omega^2L_S^2}{R_S}\\
    \therefore -\frac{1}{\omega L_P}&=-\frac{\omega L_S}{R_S^2+\omega^2L_S^2},\quad L_P=\frac{R_S^2+\omega^2L_S^2}{\omega^2L_S}
\end{align*}
Note that 
\[
    X_P=\omega L_P=\omega \left( \frac{R_S^2+\omega^2L_S^2}{\omega^2L_S} \right)=\frac{R_S^2+\left( \omega L_S \right)^2}{\omega L_S}.
\]
This means we can model the internal resistance of the inductor and the resistance of actual resistor as a single resistor, i.e.

In summary,
\begin{itemize}
    \item The quality factor of the coil is given by $Q_{\text{coil}}=\frac{\omega _0L_S}{R_S}$. Small coil resistance means large $Q_{\text{coil}}$
    \item The resonant frequency is given by $\omega _0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_PC}}$
    \item The parallel resistance is $R_P=\frac{R_S^2+\omega L_S^2}{R_S}$. Small $R_S$ means large $R_P$
    \item The parallel inductance is given by $L_P=\frac{R_S^2+\omega^2L_S^2}{\omega^2L_S}$. Small $R_S$ means $L_P\approx L_S$
    \item The overall quality factor is $Q_{\text{circuit}}=R_P\parallel R \times\omega _0\times C=\frac{R_P\parallel R}{\omega _0L_P}$
\end{itemize}

\section{Transient Analysis}
Transient analysis looks at how a circuit or network behaves over time after a sudden change, such as when a switch opens or closes. When a circuit contains energy storage elements, it does not respond instantly to these change, and instead has a transition period before reaching a steady-state condition. There are two types of circuits
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First-order circuits:} These contain only a single energy storage element, and can be described by a first order ODE.
    \item \textbf{Second-order circuits:} These contain both an inductor and a capacitor. These are described by a second order ODE.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Natural Response}
\subsubsection{Source-Free}
A source-free first-order circuit is when the transient is the independent source being switched off. The energy stored in the elements is then released through the resistor. The natural response is the behaviour of voltages and currents after the source is switched off at $t=0$.

For a source-free RC circuit, the natural response involves finding the voltage across the capacitor $C$ for $t>0$ by solving the differential equation.

For a source-free RL circuit, the natural response involves finding the current through the inductor $L$ for $t>0$ by solving the differential equation.

\subsubsection{RC Circuit}
Suppose that after a capacitor in a series RC circuit has been fully charged to $V_S$, the source is disconnected and the capacitor is allowed to discharge through the resistor. This then becomes a source-free circuit. This means to find the voltage across the capacitor, we have to solve 
\[
    \frac{v}{R}+c \frac{d v}{d t}=0\quad\Rightarrow \frac{dv}{v}=-\frac{dt}{CR}.
\]
By integrating both sides, we get
\begin{align*}
    \ln v &= -\frac{t}{RC}+\ln A,\quad \text{where }A \text{ is some constant}.\\
    \Rightarrow v(t)&=Ae^{-t/RC}\\
    v(t)&= V_S e^{-t/RC}.
\end{align*}
The current is found as 
\[
    i(t)=C \frac{d v}{d t}=-\frac{V_s}{R}e^{-t/RC}.
\]
We define the time constant $\tau$ to be $\tau =RC$. It is the time require for the response to decay to $1/e$ (about $36.8\%$) of its initial value. When $\tau $ is small it decays faster, and vice-versa. The voltage starts from $V_S$, and decays down to $0$, the current starts at $-\frac{V_S}{R}$ and decays up to $0$.

\subsubsection{RL Circuit}
If a current source is applied to a parallel RL circuit, after a sufficient amount of time all the current will pass through the inductor, and none through the resistor. The final stored energy in the inductor is given by 
\[
    w_L=\frac{1}{2}LI_S^2.
\]
Once the load is disconnected, the inductor initially resists the change in current and induces a voltage, giving 
\[
    i(0)=I_S\quad v(0)=-RI_S,
\]
when measuring across the circuit in the same polarity as the source. We then have the response of the circuit to be
\begin{align*}
    i(t)R +L \frac{d i(t)}{d t}&= 0\\
    \Rightarrow i(t)&= I_Se^{-\frac{t}{L/R}}\\
    v(t)&= L \frac{d i(t)}{d t}=-RI_Se^{-\frac{t}{L/R}}.
\end{align*}
The current starts at the initial current $I_S$, and decays down to $0$, and the voltage starts at $-RI_S$, and decays up to $0$.

\subsection{Forced Response}
A forced response is the opposite of a natural response. It is where a current or voltage is suddenly applied to the circuit, and this sudden application forces the energy in the circuit to change. The most common functions for this are the unit step, unit impulse, and unit ramp. A unit impulse and unit ramp are the derivatives and integrals of the unit step, respectively.

\subsubsection{RC Circuit}
When a DC source is suddenly applied, it can be modelled as step function and the response of the circuit is known as the step-response. We can assume that the circuit has some initial condition $v(0)=V_0$. Applying KCL,
\begin{align*}
    c \frac{d v(t)}{d t}+\frac{v(t)-V_Su(t)}{R} &= 0\\
    \frac{d v(t)}{d t}+\frac{v(t)}{RC}&=\frac{V_su(t)}{CR}\\
    \Rightarrow v(t)&=V_S+(V_0-V_S)e^{-t/RC},\quad t>0.
\end{align*}
The $V_S$ is the steady-state response, and the $(V_0-V_S)e^{-t/RC}$ is the transient response. The complete response of a circuit is the natural response (stored energy) and force response (independent source) added together 
\[
    v(t)=V_0e^{-t/RC}+V_S(1-e^{-t/RC}), \quad t>0,
\]
i.e. $v(t)=v_n(t)+v_f(t)$ (natural voltage, forced voltage).

\subsubsection{RL Circuit}
For an RL circuit, assume the inductor has zero initial stored energy, so that $i(0)=0$. Connecting current source $I_S$ causes a current, and therefore energy, buildup in the inductor. The response after this connection is given by
\begin{align*}
    I_S &= \frac{v(t)}{R}+i(t)\\
    i(t)+\frac{L}{R}\frac{d i(t)}{d t}&= I_S\\
    i(t)&=I_Se^{-\frac{t}{L/R}}\\
    v(t)&=RI_Se^{-\frac{t}{L/R}}.
\end{align*}
The current starts at $0$ and approaches $I_S$. The voltage starts at $RI_S$, and decays to 0.

\subsection{Second-Order Circuits}
A second-order circuit is one containing both inductors and capacitors. Similarly to above, the solution of the source-free RLC circuit is called the natural response of the circuit. The circuit is excited by the energy initially stored in the capacitor and inductor. The equation relating the voltage and current is given by 
\[
    \frac{d^2i}{d t^2}+\frac{R}{L}\frac{d i}{d t}+\frac{i}{LC}=0,\quad Ri+L \frac{d i}{d t}+\frac{1}{C}\int_{-\infty}^ti dt=0.
\]
We can rewrite the differential form as 
\[
    \frac{d^2i}{d t^2}+2\alpha \frac{d i}{d t}-\omega _0^2i=0,
\]
where $\alpha =\frac{R}{2L}$, and $\omega _0=\sqrt{\frac{1}{LC}}$. This means that solution depend on the relative values of $\alpha $ and $\omega _0$, where $\alpha $ is the damping factor, and $\omega _0$ is the natural frequency of the system. We can assume the solution has the form $i=Ae^{st}$, which we can substitute into the above to get
\begin{align*}
    \frac{d^2i}{d t^2}+\frac{R}{L}\frac{d i}{d t}+\frac{i}{LC} &= 0\\
    As^2e^{st}+\frac{AR}{L}se^{st}+\frac{A}{LC}e^{st}&=0\\
    \text{or}\quad Ae^{st}\left( s^2+\frac{R}{L}s +\frac{1}{LC}\right) &=0\\
    s^2+\frac{R}{L}s+\frac{1}{LC}&=0.
\end{align*}
Putting back in $\alpha $ and $\omega _0$, we get the characteristic equation 
\[
    s^2+2\alpha s+\omega^2_0=0.
\]
The roots of this equation are 
\[
    s_1=-\frac{R}{2L}+\sqrt{\left( \frac{R}{2L} \right)^2-\frac{1}{LC}},\quad s_=-\frac{R}{2L}-\sqrt{\left( \frac{R}{2L} \right)^2-\frac{1}{LC}},
\]
or 
\[
    s_1=-\alpha +\sqrt{\alpha^2-\omega^2_0},\quad s_2=-\alpha -\sqrt{\alpha^2-\omega _0^2}.
\]
These indicate two possible solutions for the current 
\[
    i_1=A_1e^{s_1t},\quad i_2=A_2e^{s_2t}.
\]
A total solution is therefore a linear combination of the two solutions 
\[
    i(t)=A_1e^{s_1t}+A_2e^{s_2t},
\]
where the constants $A_1$ and $A_2$ are determined from the initial values $i(0)$ and $\frac{d i(0)}{d t}$. This is natural response of a series RLC circuit. Note that we can find $\frac{d i(0)}{d t}$ as 
\[
    Ri(0)+L \frac{d i(0)}{d t}+v_c(0)=0,\quad\Rightarrow \frac{d i(0)}{d t}=-\frac{1}{L}\left( v_c(0)+Ri(0) \right).
\]

\subsection{Series RLC Natural Responses}
There are three types of solutions (responses) possible in a series RLC circuit
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If $\alpha >\omega _0$, the circuit is over damped 
    \[
        i(t)=A_1e^{s_1t}+A_2e^{s_2t},\quad \text{where } s_{1,2}=-\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2-\omega _0^2}.
    \]
    Both $s_1$ and $s_2$ are real and negative. The response decays to zero.
\item If $\alpha =\omega _0$, the circuit is critically damped 
\[
    i(t)=\left( A_1+A_2t \right)e^{\alpha t},\quad \text{where } s_{1,2}=-\alpha .
\]
The two roots are negative, real, and equal. The response reaches a maximum value at $\frac{1}{\alpha }$ before decaying to zero.
\item If $\alpha <\omega _0$, the circuit is under damped 
\[
    i(t)=e^{-\alpha t}\left( B_1\cos\omega _dt+B_2\sin\omega _dt \right),\quad \text{where } \omega _d=\sqrt{\omega _0^2-\alpha ^2}.
\]
Here, $B_1=A_1+B_2$, and $B_2=j(A_1-A_2)$. This response is a decaying oscillation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Under-Damped Condition}
This is when $\alpha <\omega _0$, and is a decaying oscillation. The roots of this solution are complex numbers, given by 
\[
    s_1=-\alpha +\sqrt{-\left( \omega^2_0-\alpha^2 \right)}=-\alpha +j\omega _d,\quad s_2=-\alpha -\sqrt{-\left( \omega _0^2-\alpha^2 \right)}=-\alpha -j\omega _d,
\]
where $\omega _d=\sqrt{\omega _0^2-\alpha^2}$. The response is therefore
\begin{align*}
    i(t) &= A_1e^{(-\alpha -j\omega _d)t}+A_2e^{\left( -\alpha +j\omega _d \right)t}\\
         &= e^{-\alpha t}\left( A_1e^{j\omega _dt}+A_2e^{-j\omega _dt} \right).
\end{align*}
Using Euler's identities, we get
\begin{align*}
    i(t) &= e^{-\alpha t}\left[ A_1 \left( \cos\omega _dt+j\sin\omega _dt \right) +A_2 \left( \cos\omega _dt-j\sin\omega _dt \right)\right] \\
         &= e^{-\alpha t}\left[ \left( A_1+A_2 \right)\cos\omega _dt+j(A_1-A_2)\sin\omega _dt \right] \\
         &=e^{-\alpha t}(B_1\cos\omega _dt+B_2\sin\omega _dt),
\end{align*}
where $B_1=A_1+A_2$ and $B_2=j(A_1-A_2)$.

\subsection{Series RLC Forced Response}
The step response is obtained by the sudden application of a DC source to an RLC circuit 
\[
    L \frac{d i}{d t}+Ri+v=V_s,\quad i=C \frac{d v}{d t}.
\]
This gives the expression 
\[
    \frac{d^2v(t)}{d t^2}+\frac{R}{L}\frac{d v(t)}{d t}+\frac{d v(t)}{d LC}=\frac{V_s}{LC}.
\]
This is of the same form as the source-free RLC circuit equation above, with the same coefficients. The only difference is different circuit variables in the equation. The solution of the equation should have two components, the transient response $v_t$ and the steady-state response $v_{ss}(t)$ 
\[
    v(t)=v_t(t)+v_{ss}(t).
\]
The transient response (as in the first-order case) is the same as that for the source-free case (natural response). The steady-state response is the final value of $v(t)$, i.e. $v_{ss}(t)=v(\infty)$.

\subsection{Parallel RLC Natural Response}
Let the initial current 
\[
    i(0)=I_0=\frac{1}{L}\int_{-\infty}^0v(t)dt, \quad v(0)=V_0.
\]
Applying KCL to the top node, we get 
\[
    \frac{v}{R}+\frac{1}{L}\int_{-\infty}^tvdt+C \frac{d v}{d t}=0.
\]
Taking the derivative with respect to $t$ and dividing by $C$, 
\[
    \frac{d^2v}{d t^2}+\frac{1}{RC}\frac{dv}{dt}+\frac{1}{RC}v=0,
\]
which is the second-order expression for a parallel RLC circuit. As before, there are three possible types of solutions for this differential equation 
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $\alpha >\omega _0$, the system is over-damped, $v(t)=A_1e^{s_1t}+A_2e^{s_2t}$, where $s_{1,2}=-\alpha \pm\sqrt{\alpha^2-\omega^2_0}$
    \item If $\alpha =\omega _0$, the system is critically damped, $v(t)=\left( A_2+A_1t \right)e^{-\alpha t}$, where $s_{1,2}=-\alpha $
    \item If $\alpha <\omega _0$, the system is under-damped, $v(t)=e^{-\alpha t}\left( B_1\cos\omega _dt+B_2\sin\omega _dt \right),$ where $\omega _d=\sqrt{\omega _0^2-\alpha ^2}$ and 
    \[
        s_{1,2}=-\alpha \pm \sqrt{-\left( \omega _0^2-\alpha ^2 \right)}=-\alpha \pm j\omega _d.
    \]
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Parallel RLC Forced Response}
The step response is obtained by the sudden application of a DC source to the circuit, giving the second-order expression 
\[
    \frac{d^2i}{d t^2}+\frac{1}{RC}\frac{d i}{d t}+\frac{i}{RC}=\frac{I_S}{LC}.
\]
It has the same form as the equation for the source-free parallel RLC circuit, with the same coefficients. The difference is different circuit variables. The solution should have two components, the transient response $i_t(t)$, and the steady-state response $i_{ss}(t)$ 
\[
    i_(t)=\begin{cases}
        A_1^{s_1t}+A_2e^{s_2t} & \alpha >\omega _0 \\
        \left( A_1+A_2t \right)e^{-\alpha t} & \alpha =\omega _0\\
        e^{-\alpha t}\left( A_1 \cos\omega _dt+A_2\sin\omega _dt \right) & \alpha <\omega _0
    \end{cases}.
\]
The steady-state response is the final value of $i(t)$ 
\[
    i_{ss}(t)=i(\infty)=I_S.
\]
The values of $A_1$ and $A_2$ are obtained from the initial conditions $i(0)$ and $\frac{d i(0)}{d t}$.

\section{Filters}
\subsection{Types of Filters}
The following are idealised specifications for the most common filter functions

\noindent\textbf{Ideal Low-Pass Filter}

Passes low frequencies, blocks high frequencies.
\[
    |H(j\omega )|=\begin{cases}
        1, & 0\leq\omega \leq\omega _c \\
        0, & \omega >\omega _c
    \end{cases}
\]
\indent Passband: $0\leq\omega <\omega _c$, stopband: $\omega _c<\omega \leq\infty$.

\noindent \textbf{Ideal High-Pass Filter}

Passes high frequencies, blocks low frequencies.
\[
    |H(j\omega )|=\begin{cases}
        0, & 0\leq\omega <\omega _c \\
        1, & \omega >\omega _c
    \end{cases}
\]
\indent Passband: $\omega _c<\omega \leq\infty$, stopband: $0\leq\omega <\omega _c$.

\noindent \textbf{Ideal Band-Pass Filter}

Passes only frequencies between $\omega _L$ and $\omega _H$.
\[
    |H(j\omega )|=\begin{cases}
        1, & \omega _L\leq\omega \leq\omega _H \\
        0, & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\]
\indent Passband: $\omega _L\leq\omega \leq\omega _H$, stopbands: $\omega <\omega _L$ and $\omega >\omega _H$.

\noindent \textbf{Ideal Band-Stop (Band-Reject) Filter}

Rejects frequencies between $\omega _L$ and $\omega _H$.
\[
    |H(j\omega )|=\begin{cases}
        0, & \omega _L\leq\omega _H \\
        1, & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\]
\indent Passbands: $\omega <\omega _L$ and $\omega >\omega _H$, stopband: $\omega _L<\omega <\omega _H$.

\section{Useful Integrals}
\subsection{Integrals}
For integer $n\neq0$
\begin{align*}
    \int\sin(ax) dx&= -\frac{1}{a}\cos(ax) \\
    \int\cos(ax)dx&= \frac{1}{a}\sin(ax) \\
    \int_0^T\sin(n\omega _0t)dt&=0 \\
    \int_0^T\cos(n\omega _0t)dt&=0
\end{align*}

\subsection{Integration by Parts}
\subsubsection{Key Formula}
Key formula:
\[
    \int u dv=uv-\int vdu.
\]
\begin{align*}
    \int x e^{ax}dx &= \frac{e^{ax}}{a^2}(ax-1) \\
    \int x^2e^{ax}dx&=\frac{e^{ax}}{a^3}(a^2x^2-2ax+2)
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Cosine}
Key formula:
\[
    \int t\cos(n\omega _0t)dt.
\]
Let $u=t\Rightarrow dt$, $dv=\cos(n\omega _0t)dt\Rightarrow v=\frac{1}{n\omega _0}\sin(n\omega _0t)$
\[
    \boxed{\int t\cos(n\omega _0t)dt=\frac{1}{n\omega _0}\sin(n\omega _0t)-\frac{1}{\left( n\omega _0 \right)^2}\cos(n\omega _0t)+G}
\]

\subsubsection{Sine}
Key formula:
\[
    \int t\sin(n\omega _0t)dt.
\]
Let $u=t\Rightarrow du=dt$, $dv=\sin(n\omega _0t)dt\Rightarrow v=-\frac{1}{n\omega _0}\cos(n\omega _0t)$
\[
    \boxed{\int t\sin(n\omega _0t)dt=-\frac{t}{n\omega _0}\cos(n\omega _0t)+\frac{1}{\left( n\omega _0 \right)^2}\sin(n\omega _0t)+G}
\]

\subsubsection{Definite Integration by Parts}
For $0\leq t\leq T$ and integer $n$
\[
    \int_0^Tt\sin(n\omega _0t)dt=\boxed{\frac{T}{n\omega _0}}\quad \text{and}\quad \int_0^Tt\cos(n\omega_0t)dt=\boxed{0}.
\]

\subsection{Definite Integrals}
$x^2$ and trigonometric function:
\begin{align*}
    \int_0^T x^2\cos(nx)dx&=\left[ \frac{x^2}{n}\sin(nx)+\frac{2x}{n^2}\cos(nx)-\frac{2}{n^3}\sin(nx) \right]_0^T \\
    \int_0^Tx^2\sin(nx)dx&= \left[ -\frac{x^2}{n}\cos(nx)+\frac{2x}{n^2}\sin(nx)+\frac{2}{n^3}\cos(nx) \right]_0^T.
\end{align*}

$x$ and trigonometric function:
\begin{align*}
    \int_0^Tx\cos(nx)dx &= \left[ \frac{x}{n}\sin(nx)+\frac{1}{n^2}\cos(nx) \right]_0^T \\
    \int_0^Tx\sin(nx)dx&=\left[ -\frac{x}{n}\cos(nx)+\frac{1}{n^2}\sin(nx) \right]_0^T.
\end{align*}

Miscellaneous:
\begin{align*}
    \int_0^T\cos(m\omega _0t)\sin(n\omega _0t)dt&=0\quad \text{for all m and n} \\
    \int_0^T\sin(m\omega _0t)\sin(n\omega _0t)dt&=\begin{cases}
        0 & \text{for all }m\neq n \\
        \frac{T}{2} & \text{for } m=n
    \end{cases} \\
    \int_0^T\cos(m\omega _0t)\cos(n\omega _0t)dt&= \begin{cases}
        0 & \text{for all }m \neq n \\
        \frac{T}{2} & \text{for } m=n
    \end{cases}.
\end{align*}

\section{Trigonometric Identities}
\begin{align*}
    \sin(\alpha \pm\beta ) &= \sin\alpha \cos\beta \pm\cos\alpha \sin\beta  \\
    \cos(\alpha \pm\beta ) &=\cos\alpha \cos\beta \mp\sin\alpha \sin\beta \\
    \cos(\alpha \pm 90\degree)&= \mp\sin\alpha \\
    \sin(\alpha \pm90\degree)&= \pm\cos\alpha \\
    \cos\alpha \cos\beta &= \frac{1}{2}\cos(\alpha +\beta )+\frac{1}{2}\cos(\alpha -\beta )\\
    \sin\alpha \sin\beta &= \frac{1}{2}\cos(\alpha -\beta )-\frac{1}{2}\cos(\alpha +\beta ) \\
    \sin\alpha \cos\beta &=\frac{1}{2}\sin(\alpha +\beta)+\frac{1}{2}\sin(\alpha -\beta )\\
    \sin 2\alpha &=2\sin\alpha \cos\alpha .
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
    \cos 2\alpha  &= 2\cos^2\alpha -1 \\
                  &=1-2\sin^2\alpha \\
                  &=\cos^2\alpha -\sin^2\alpha \\
    \sin^2\alpha &=\frac{1}{2}(1-\cos 2\alpha )\\
    \cos^2\alpha &=\frac{1}{2}(1+\cos 2\alpha ) \\
    \sin\alpha&= \frac{e^{j\alpha }-e^{-j\alpha }}{2j}\\
    \cos\alpha&=\frac{e^{j\alpha }+e^{-j\alpha }}{2} \\
    e^{\pm j\alpha }&=\cos\alpha \pm j\sin\alpha  \\
    A\cos\alpha +B\sin\alpha &=\sqrt{A^2+B^2}\cos \left( \alpha +\tan^{-1}\left( \frac{B}{A} \right) \right).
\end{align*}
