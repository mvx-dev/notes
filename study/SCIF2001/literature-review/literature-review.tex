\section{Literature Review}
Quantum mechanics predicts correlations between measurements on entangled systems that cannot be reproduced by assigning independent, pre-existing properties to each particle. This issue was notably highlighted by the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen argument (also know as the EPR paradox) \parencite{einsteinCanQuantumMechanicalDescription1935}, and converted into an experimentally testable prediction by Bell. Bell showed that any theory satisfying local realism must obey an upper bound on measurable correlations, whereas quantum mechanics predicts violations for suitable entangled states \parencite{bellEinsteinPodolskyRosen1964}.

The Clauser-Horne-Shimony-Holt (CHSH) formulation made Bell's theorem practical for laboratory experiments. In a polarization experiment, two measurement settings are selected on each photon path in a entangled photon-pair setup, $a$ and $a'$ for one photon, and $b$ and $b'$ for the other. Each polarisation measurement has two possible outcomes. For each pair of settings, the correlation can be calculated from the coincidence counts
\[
    E(a,b)=\frac{N_{++}+N_{--}-N_{+-}-N_{-+}}{N_{++}+N_{--}+N_{+-}+N_{-+}}.
\]
The CHSH parameter is then
\[
    S=\left| E(a,b)-E(a,b')+E(a',b)+E(a'+b') \right|.
\]
Local hidden-variable theories require $S\leq 2$, while quantum mechanics predicts values as large as $2\sqrt{2}$ for an ideal maximally entangled state \parencite{clauserProposedExperimentTest1969}. Therefore, a measured value significantly greater than 2 would provide evidence that the observed correlations cannot be explain by a local hidden-variable model.

Optical experiments provide a direct way to test this prediction. An entangled photon-pair source sends on photon into each measurement arm, with the measurements defined by polarisers. Any correlation events within the same coincidence window are detected by a correlation device, however this detects classically correlated photons as they may produce similar measurement outcomes. This is not an issue as only sufficiently strong correlations that violate the CHSH bound demonstrate Bell nonlocality under the assumption of the experiment.

Another demonstration of the quantum nature of light is photon antibunching. The quantum theory of optical coherence describes multi-photon detection using correlation functions (functions describing the statistically correlation between random variables), most notably the second-order correlation function $g^{(2)}(\tau )$ \parencite{glauberQuantumTheoryOptical1963}. At zero delay, $g^{(2)}(0)$ measures the relative probability of detecting two photons close together in time.

For ideal coherent light $g^{(2)}(0)=1$, corresponding to Poissonian detection statistics. Thermal light commonly produces $g^{(2)}>1$, known as photon bunching. In contrast, antibunched light satisfies
\[
    g^{(2)}(0)<1,
\]
indicating a suppression of near-simultaneous detections. The observation of anti bunching in resonance fluorescence was a significant demonstration of nonclassical light because the measured statistics could not be explained by a simple classical fluctuating-intensity model \parencite{kimblePhotonAntibunchingResonance1977}.

A related experiment used a triggered photon source and a beam splitter to observe strong anticorrelation between the two output detectors. Their result was consistent with a single-photon state and inconsistent with the prediction of a classical wave model for the relevant experiment conditions \parencite{grangierExperimentalEvidencePhoton1986}. These experiments establish that time-correlated detection measurements can provide a practical test nonclassical optical behaviour.

\subsection{Laboratory Skills}
During the couple of weeks leading up to this literature review, I, along with another student, developed the skills necessary to work with optical fibres in a laboratory environment. This included fibre preparation, laser alignment, and characterising both the quality of the alignment, as well as some properties of the fibre itself.

The first stage of preparing a length of fibre is stripping off the outer-most protective layer, as well as the cladding. After cleaning the remaining cladding off of the fibre using acetone, we utilised a microscope connected to a computer in order to verify that all material was removed from around the core of the fibre. After this, we used a cleaver to make a clean, controlled break in the fibre, which we also checked under the microscope (see Figs. \ref{fig:cleaved-good}, \ref{fig:cleaved-bad}). If we learnt one thing during our time working with fibres, it is that any sort of defect or nick or chip in the end of the fibre would significantly degrade the quality of the laser entering the fibre, which would in turn impact any experiment we may undertake. Whilst in this setup the light in even a bad cleave would be sufficient to do an experiment with, in the more advanced setups in the future, this loss would correspond to a significant proportion of the usable photons.

Once we had a good length of fibre, we were able to align the tip of the fibre to laser light going through a microscope objective. This stage was by far both the most important part, and the most tedious. This involved using adjustment screws in all of the $x$, $y$, and $z$ axes, which allowed the tip of the laser to be placed as close as possible to both the brightest region of laser light, as well as the correct distance from the microscope objective to ensure the light is focused. Initially, we ran into issues with the tip of the fibre being slightly bent due to storage on a reel. We were able to correct for this by having a shorter length of the fibre protruding from the holder, and moving the objective closer to account for this loss of range in the $z$-axis.

This laser alignment was aided by the use of light power meter. The end of the optical fibre not aligned with the laser was directed at a photosensor, which was read by the power meter to quantify exactly how much light, in terms of Watts, was being carried through the fibre. From this, we were able to see the difference in power whilst aligning, as can be seen in Figs. \ref{fig:power-good} and \ref{fig:power-not-so-good}.

\section{Proposed Methodology}
This experiment is broken down into two stages. The first is a proof-of-concept experiment, where we can verify the optical alignment and timing system, and the second is the actual Bell's inequality violation.

\subsection{Correlation Measurement}
The proof-of-concept measurement will measure photon antibunching, which will allow us to verify the optical alignment of the setup, as well as test the timing system. The photon source will be configured to produce a herald signal and a corresponding photon in the measurement arm. Detector dark counts and background counts will first be measured with the source blocked to provide a baselines measurement, then the source will be operated for repeated acquisition intervals while the correlation device records the distribution of detection delays.

The primary output will be a histogram of detection-delay events. The coincidence peak will be identified and compared with the surrounding accidental-coincidence background recorded previously. This measured coincidence rate can then be used to estimate the correlation function $g^{(2)}(\tau )$, and evidence of antibunching would be indicated by a statistically significant reduction in the zero-delay coincidence rate relative to the long-delay baseline.

This provides three checks for the setup:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Whether the detectors produce stable count rate
    \item Whether the correlation device resolve the relevant timing structure
    \item Whether the chosen coincidence window is narrow enough to distinguish actual events from accidental coincidences.
\end{enumerate}
If a direct antibunching measurement is not possible with the available source, the fallback milestone will be a reproducible heralded-pair coincidence peak. This does not demonstrate antibunching, however it validates the timing, alignment, and detector-correlation pipeline needed for the Bell experiment.
