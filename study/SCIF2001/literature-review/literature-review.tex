\section{Literature Review}
Quantum mechanics predicts correlations between measurements on entangled systems that cannot be reproduced by assigning independent, pre-existing properties to each particle. This issue was notably highlighted by the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen argument, which questioned whether quantum mechanics provides a complete description of physical reality \parencite{einsteinCanQuantumMechanicalDescription1935}. Bell later converted this into an experimentally testable problem by showing that any theory satisfying local realism must obey an upper bound on measurable correlations, whereas quantum mechanics predicts violations for suitable entangled states \parencite{bellEinsteinPodolskyRosen1964}.

The Clauser-Horne-Shimony-Holt (CHSH) formulation made Bell's theorem practical for laboratory experiments. In a polarisation experiment, two measurement settings are selected on each photon path in a entangled photon-pair setup, $a$ and $a'$ for one photon, and $b$ and $b'$ for the other. Each polarisation measurement has two possible outcomes, conventionally labelled $+$ and $-$. For a pair of settings, the correlation is calculated from the coincidence counts as
\begin{equation}
    E(a,b)=\frac{N_{++}+N_{--}-N_{+-}-N_{-+}}{N_{++}+N_{--}+N_{+-}+N_{-+}}. \label{eq:1}
\end{equation}
The CHSH parameter is then
\begin{equation}
    S=\left| E(a,b)-E(a,b')+E(a',b)+E(a',b') \right|.\label{eq:2}
\end{equation}
Local hidden-variable theories require $S\leq 2$, while quantum mechanics predicts values as large as $2\sqrt{2}$ for an ideal maximally entangled state \parencite{clauserProposedExperimentTest1969}. Therefore, a measured value significantly greater than 2 would provide evidence that the observed correlations cannot be explained by a local hidden-variable model. Correlation alone is not enough, since classically correlated sources can also produce similar outcomes. The relevant criteria is the violation of the CHSH bound.

Optical experiments provide a direct way to test this prediction. An entangled photon-pair source sends one photon into each measurement arm, with the measurement settings are defined by polarisers. A correlation device identifies detections occurring within a selected coincidence window, allowing the coincidence counts for each outcome to be measured. Early optical experiments established the feasibility of this approach. Freedman and Clauser measured linear-polarisation correlations from photons emitted in an atomic cascade and found results consistent with quantum mechanics, but inconsistent with local hidden-variable restrictions \parencite{freedmanExperimentalTestLocal1972}. Aspect, Dalibard, and Roger later measured photon-polarisation correlations using time-varying analysers and reported a violation of Bell's inequality \parencite{aspectExperimentalTestBells1982}. These experiments provide the experimental precedent for the proposed use of an entangled source, polarisers, and coincidence detectors.

A separate demonstration of the quantum nature of light is photon antibunching. The quantum theory of optical coherence describes multi-photon detection using correlation functions, including the second-order correlation function $g^{(2)}(\tau )$ \parencite{glauberQuantumTheoryOptical1963}. At zero delay, $g^{(2)}(0)$ measures the relative probability of detecting two photons close temporally. Ideal coherent light $g^{(2)}(0)=1$, corresponding to Poissonian detection statistics, while thermal light generally exhibits bunching with $g^{(2)}(0)>1$. Antibunched light satisfies $g^{(2)}(0)<1$, indicating a suppression of near-simultaneous detections. The observation of antibunching in resonance fluorescence by Kimble, Dagenais, and Mandel was an important demonstration of nonclassical light because the measured photon statistics could not be explained by the relevant classical fluctuating-intensity models \parencite{kimblePhotonAntibunchingResonance1977}.

Grangier, Roger, and Aspect demonstrated a related effect using a triggered photon source and a beam splitter. They observed strong anticorrelation between the two output detectors, consistent with a single-photon description and inconsistent with a classical wave model under the conditions of their experiment \parencite{grangierExperimentalEvidencePhoton1986}. These results show that photon-timing measurements can provide practical evidence of nonclassical optical behaviour. However, there is an important distinction between actual photon antibunching measurements and a simple photon-pair correlation measurement. A reproducible coincidence peak from a heralded source demonstrates temporal correlation between the photons, but does not by itself establish $g^{(2)}(0)<1$.

Modern loophole-free photon experiments require significant detector efficiency, independently selected measurement settings and additional control of locality and coincidence loopholes \parencite{giustinaSignificantLoopholeFreeTestBells2015a}. This means that due to the limitations of the equipment available, this experiment will be interpreted as a CHSH test under the equipment's detection, coincidence selection, and fair sampling assumptions.

\subsection{Proof of Concept}
The first stage of the experiment will test the performance of the source, detectors, and correlation device. The entangled photon-pair source will be configured so that one detected photon provides a herald and the corresponding photon is sent to a second detector. The optical paths will initially be aligned using the available general optical equipment.

Before collecting the source data, detector dark counts and background counts will be measured with the source blocked. This will provide a baseline for estimating accidental coincidences. The source will then be operated for repeated acquisition intervals while the correlation device records the time difference between detection events.

The primary output will be histogram of detection delays. An actual heralded-pair signal should produce a coincidence peak above the surrounding accidental coincidence background. The peak position and width will be used to selecct an appropriate coincidence window. Repeated measurements will test whether the count rates and coincidence peak remains stable.

This provides three checks for the setup:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Whether the detectors produce stable count rate
    \item Whether the correlation device resolve the relevant timing structure
    \item Whether the chosen coincidence window is narrow enough to distinguish actual events from accidental coincidences.
\end{enumerate}

If the optical arrangement allows for a $g^{(2)}(\tau )$ measurement, the normalised second-order correlation function will also be estimated. If the measured zero-delay value satisfies $g^{(2)}(0)<1$, then we can say the photons are antibunched.

\subsection{Polarisation-Correlation and CHSH Experiment}
After the timing system has been validated, the two photons from the entangled source will be directed into separate measurement arms. Polarisers will be installed in both arms, with their angular positions recorded relative to a common reference, then split with a PBS.

Initial measurements will be performed at several analyser angles to verify the presence and orientation of the expected polarisation correlations. This step will also identify whether the source produces correlations described by $E(\alpha ,\beta )$ or by the corresponding sign-reversed convention for the particular entangled state.

At each setting, the experiment will record detector count rate, coincidence counts, and the acquisition time. Background and accidental coincidences will be estimated either from off-peak timing windows or from measurements with one arm blocked, depending on the capabilities of the correlation device. The source alignment will be adjusted before collecting the final CHSH data.

The CHSH experiment will use two analyser settings on each measurement arm
\[
    (a,a'),\quad(b,b').
\]

The four analyser-setting combinations
\[
    (a,b),\quad(a,b'),\quad(a',b),\quad(a',b')
\]
will be measured. For each pair, the coincidence counts for the available $+$ and $-$ outcome channels will be collected over a fixed integration time. The correlation will then be calculated using Equation \ref{eq:1}, which the four correlations will be substituted into Equation \ref{eq:2}.

Because only three single-photon detectors are available, all four outcomes channels may not be recorded simultaneously. To combat this, a polariser will be placed in the $B$ arm, and measurements will be inferred. This will introduce additional assumptions about detector stability and fair sampling, which is why this experiment is not claiming to be loophole-free.

Repeated acquisitions will be performed for each analyser setting. For approximately independent Poissonian counts, the uncertainty in an individual count will initially be estimated as
\[
    \sigma _N\approx\sqrt{N}.
\]
These uncertainties will be propagated through the correlation and CHSH expressions to obtain
\[
    S=S_{\text{meas}}+\sigma _S.
\]
A violation will be considered meaningful only if $S_{\text{meas}}>2$ by a statistically significant margin and the result is reasonably consistent across repeated acquisitions.

\subsection{Limitations and Contingency}
The main experimental risks are low photon-pair rates, detector inefficiency, accidental coincidences, unstable optical alignment, and incomplete access to the four required outcome channels.

If the coincidence peak is weak, the alignment will be checked and the integration time increased. If accidental coincidences are significant, the coincidence window will be reduced where this does not exclude actual photon pairs. If the result varies between repeated measurements, the source alignment, fibre coupling, and detector count rates will be monitored before additional data are collected.

If the apparatus cannot provide all outcome channels required for a complete CHSH analysis, the project will report the available polarisation-correlation visibility and compare its angular dependence with the quantum prediction. If the CHSH parameter cannot be estimated reliably, the heralded-correlation proof of concept will remain the primary experimental outcome.
