\section{Literature Review}
Quantum mechanics predicts correlations between measurements on entangled systems that cannot be reproduced by assigning independent, pre-existing properties to each particle. This issue was notably highlighted by the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen argument (also know as the EPR paradox) \parencite{einsteinCanQuantumMechanicalDescription1935}, and converted into an experimentally testable prediction by Bell. Bell showed that any theory satisfying local realism must obey an upper bound on measurable correlations, whereas quantum mechanics predicts violations for suitable entangled states \parencite{bellEinsteinPodolskyRosen1964}.

The Clauser-Horne-Shimony-Holt (CHSH) formulation made Bell's theorem practical for laboratory experiments. In a polarization experiment, two measurement settings are selected on each photon path in a entangled photon-pair setup, $a$ and $a'$ for one photon, and $b$ and $b'$ for the other. For each pair of settings, the correlation can be calculated from the coincidence counts
\[
    E(a,b)=\frac{N_{++}+N_{--}-N_{+-}-N_{-+}}{N_{++}+N_{--}+N_{+-}+N_{-+}}.
\]
The CHSH parameter is then
\[
    S=\left| E(a,b)-E(a,b')+E(a',b)+E(a'+b') \right|.
\]
Local hidden-variable theories require $S\leq 2$, while quantum mechanics predicts values as large as $2\sqrt{2}$ for an ideal maximally entangled state \parencite{clauserProposedExperimentTest1969}. Therefore, a measured value significantly greater than 2 would provide evidence that the observed correlations cannot be explained by a local hidden-variable model.

Optical experiments provide a direct way to test this prediction. An entangled photon-pair source sends one photon into each measurement arm, with the measurements defined by polarisers. Any correlation events within the same coincidence window are detected by a correlation device, however this could detect classically correlated photons also. This is not an issue, as only strong correlations that violate the CHSH bound demonstrate Bell nonlocality,.

Another demonstration of the quantum nature of light is photon antibunching. The quantum theory of optical coherence describes multi-photon detection using correlation functions, most notably the second-order correlation function $g^{(2)}(\tau )$ \parencite{glauberQuantumTheoryOptical1963}. At zero delay, $g^{(2)}(0)$ measures the relative probability of detecting two photons close temporally.

For ideal coherent light $g^{(2)}(0)=1$, corresponding to Poissonian detection statistics. Thermal light commonly produces $g^{(2)}>1$, known as photon bunching. In contrast, antibunched light satisfies $g^{(2)}(0)<1$, indicating a suppression of near-simultaneous detections. The observation of anti bunching in resonance fluorescence was a significant demonstration of nonclassical light as measured statistics could not be explained by a simple classical fluctuating-intensity model \parencite{kimblePhotonAntibunchingResonance1977}.

A related experiment used a triggered photon source and a beam splitter to observe strong anticorrelation between the two output detectors. Their result was consistent with a single-photon state and inconsistent with the prediction of a classical wave model for the relevant experiment conditions \parencite{grangierExperimentalEvidencePhoton1986}. These experiments establish that time-correlated detection measurements can provide a practical test nonclassical optical behaviour.

During the weeks leading up to this literature review, I, along with another student, developed the skills necessary to work with optical fibres in a laboratory environment. This included fibre preparation, laser alignment, and characterising both the quality of the alignment, as well as some properties of the fibre itself.

The first stage of preparing a length of fibre is stripping off the outer-most protective layer, as well as the cladding. After cleaning the remaining cladding off of the fibre using acetone, we utilised a microscope to verify that all material was removed from the core of the fibre. After this, we used a cleaver to make a clean, controlled break in the fibre (Fig. \ref{fig:cleaved-good}, \ref{fig:cleaved-bad}). The most important thing we learnt during our time working with fibres, it is that any sort of defect in the end of the fibre would significantly degrade the amount of light entering, which would in turn impact any experiment we may undertake. Whilst in this setup the light in even a bad cleave would be sufficient to do an experiment with, in the more advanced setups in the future, this loss would correspond to a significant portion of the usable photons.

\section{Proposed Methodology}
This experiment is broken down into two stages. The first is a proof-of-concept experiment, where we can verify the optical alignment and timing system, and the second is the actual Bell's inequality violation.

\subsection{Correlation Measurement}
The proof-of-concept measurement will measure photon antibunching, which will allow us to verify the optical alignment of the setup, as well as test the timing system. The photon source will be configured to produce a herald signal and a corresponding photon in the measurement arm. Detector dark counts and background counts will first be measured with the source blocked to provide a baselines measurement, then the source will be operated for repeated acquisition intervals while the correlation device records the distribution of detection delays.

The primary output will be a histogram of detection-delay events. The coincidence peak will be identified and compared with the surrounding accidental-coincidence background recorded previously. This measured coincidence rate can then be used to estimate the correlation function $g^{(2)}(\tau )$, and evidence of antibunching would be indicated by a statistically significant reduction in the zero-delay coincidence rate relative to the long-delay baseline.

This provides three checks for the setup:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Whether the detectors produce stable count rate
    \item Whether the correlation device resolve the relevant timing structure
    \item Whether the chosen coincidence window is narrow enough to distinguish actual events from accidental coincidences.
\end{enumerate}
If a direct antibunching measurement is not possible with the available source, the fallback milestone will be a reproducible heralded-pair coincidence peak. This does not demonstrate antibunching, however it validates the timing, alignment, and detector-correlation pipeline needed for the Bell experiment.

\subsection{Polarisation-Correlation and CHSH Experiment}
After the timing system has been validated, the two photons from the entangled source will be directed into separate measurement arms. Polarisers will be installed in both arms, with their angular positions recorded relative to a common reference. Initial measurements will be performed at several analyser angles to verify high-visibility polarisation correlations and to identify the orientation convention of the generated entangled state.

At each setting, the experiment will record detector count rate, coincidence counts, and the acquisition time. Background and accidental coincidences will be estimated either from off-peak timing windows or from measurements with one arm blocked, depending on the capabilities of the correlation device. The source alignment will be adjusted before collecting the final CHSH data.

For the CHSH data, the four analyser-setting combinations
\[
    (a,b),\quad(a,b'),\quad(a',b),\quad(a',b')
\]
will be measured. For each combination, the coincidence counts for the available outcome channels will be collected over a fixed integration time. A common ideal choice of relative analyser angles gives a quantum prediction near the maximum CHSH expression to obtain $S$. Repeated acquisitions will be used to estimate the variation in $S$, and the uncertainty will be calculated from counting statistics. For independent Poissonian counts, a first estimate of the uncertainty can be obtained by propagating the count uncertainties, with each count approximated by
\[
    \sigma _N\approx \sqrt{N}.
\]
The final result will be of the form
\[
    S=S_{\text{meas}}\pm\sigma _S.
\]
For a violation measurement to be valid would require $S_{\text{meas}}>2$ by an uncertainty margin that is statistically meaningful. The analysis will also report the coincidence window, integration time, detector count rate, accidental-coincidence treatment and any postselection or fair-sampling assumpting.

\subsection{Limitations and Contingency}
The main technical risks to this experiment are detector efficiency at the photon-pair wavelength, unstable optical alignment, accidental coincidences, low pair-production rates, and incomplete access to the four required polarisation outcomes. If the measured correlations are too weak for statistically significant violation, the project will first repeat the visibility and timing measurement, then optimise alignment and coincidence-window selection.

If the three detector arrangement prevents simultaneous measurement of all four outcome combinations, the measurement will be performed sequentially or with the complementary outcome inferred from the available count information. If a complete CHSH analysis remains impractical, we will report the polarisation-correlation visibility and compare the measured angular dependence with the quantum prediction. The photon antibunching milestone will remain a successful proof of concept for the detection and timing system.
