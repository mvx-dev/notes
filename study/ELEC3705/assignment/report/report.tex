\section{Introduction}
In this assignment, we simulate Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) imaging of a two-dimensional grayscale image, where the value of each pixel is the number of hydrogen nuclei at that location. A strong static field $B_z$, whose magnitude depends on the column index, is combined with a uniform oscillating field $B_x(t)$ at a fixed frequency. Only columns whose Larmor frequency is close to the drive frequency are strongly excited. By moving the centre of the field profile across the image and recording the total signal of each row, the image can be reconstructed column by column.

The nuclei do not interact with each other, so each pixel is modelled as one isolated spin-$\tfrac12$, with the number of nuclei in the pixel acting as a multiplicative factor. The Hamiltonian is $\mathcal{H}=\gamma _n\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{I}$, with $\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{\sigma }/2$, and $\gamma _n=42.48\MHz/\text{T}$, and it is expressed in Hz. The Schr\"odinger equation is then
\[
    i \frac{d \ket{\psi }}{d t}=2\pi \mathcal{H}\ket{\psi },
\]
so for a time-independent Hamiltonian the time evolution operator is
\begin{equation}
    U(t)=e^{-i2\pi \mathcal{H}t}. \label{eq:1}
\end{equation}
The factor of $2\pi $ is the conversion $h/\hbar$.

\section{Question 1.1: Image Size}
After loading the image, the shape of the array is $255 \times 255$. Note that $c=0$ corresponds to the centre pixel of the image, which in this case is $127$.

\section{Question 1.2: Zeeman Energies}
The static field is a Gaussian dip in the column coordinate, given by
\begin{align}
    B_z(c) &= 1-\frac{G(c)}{2\max G(c)}\ \text{T},\label{eq:Bz} \\
    G(c) &= \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi }}e^{-(c-\mu )^2/2\sigma ^2},\quad\sigma =10.
\end{align}
Since $\max G=G(\mu )=\frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi }}$, the ratio is $\frac{G}{2\max G}=\frac{1}{2}e^{-(c-\mu )^2/2\sigma ^2}$, so $B_z=1\ \text{T}$ far from $\mu $, and $B_z=0.5\ \text{T}$ at $c=\mu $. The Hamiltonian in this field is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}=\gamma B_zI_z\quad I_z=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}
        1&0\\0&-1
    \end{pmatrix},\label{eq:Hz}
\end{equation}
which is diagonal with eigenvalues $\lambda =\pm\gamma _nB_z/2$. The two spin levels are therefore split by
\begin{equation}
    E_z=\gamma _nB_z,
\end{equation}
which is the Larmor frequency. Fig. \ref{fig:zeeman} shows $E_z(c)$ for the field centred at the middle pixel and shifted 20 pixels to the right. It is an inverted Gaussian, falling from $42.48\MHz$ far from the centre to $21.24\MHz$ at $c=\mu $.

\section{Question 1.3: Hamiltonian of Each Pixel}
With $B_x=B_y=0$, only the $I_z$ term of $\gamma _n\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{I}$ has an effect. Substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:Bz} into Eq. \eqref{eq:Hz} and centring the field on the middle pixel ($\mu =0$),
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}(c)=\gamma _n \left( 1-\frac{1}{2}e^{-c^2/200} \right)I_z.
\end{equation}
The Hamiltonian is diagonal because there is no transverse field yet. For the central column ($c=0$),
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}(0)=\frac{1}{2}\gamma I_z=21.24\MHz \cdot I_z=\begin{pmatrix}
        10.62&0\\0&-10.62
    \end{pmatrix}\MHz,
\end{equation}
and for the last column ($c=127$), where $e^{-127^2/200}\approx 10^{-35}$ is negligible,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}(127)\approx\gamma _nI_z=42.48\MHz \cdot I_z=\begin{pmatrix}
        21.24&0\\0&-21.24
    \end{pmatrix}\MHz.
\end{equation}
The diagonal entries are half the Larmor frequency because $I_z$ has eigenvalues $\pm \frac{1}{2}$. Every pixel in a column shares the same Hamiltonian, so we store one $2\times  2$ matrix per column.

\section{Question 1.4: Rabi Frequency and the Rotating Frame}
We now add the oscillating field $B_x(t)=B_{amp}\cos(2\pi ft)$ with $B_{amp}=50 \text{mT}$. The laboratory-frame Hamiltonian of column $c$ is given by
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}(t)=f_L(c) I_z+\gamma _nB_{amp}\cos(2\pi ft)I_x,\quad f_L(c)=\gamma _nB_z(c).
\end{equation}
A linearly oscillating field is the sum of two fields rotating in opposite directions about $z$,
\begin{equation}
    B_x=B_{amp} \frac{e^{i 2\pi ft}+e^{-i 2\pi ft}}{2},
\end{equation}
each of amplitude $\frac{B_{amp}}{2}$. The component $e^{i 2\pi ft}$ rotates with the precessing spins and drives them at resonance. The component $e^{-i 2\pi ft}$ is detuned by $2f$, and so has not effect (this is rotating-wave approximation).

The drive frequency was chosen to be equal to the Larmor frequency of the central column,
\begin{equation}
    f=f_L(0)=\gamma _n(0.5\ \text{T})=21.24\MHz.
\end{equation}
Transforming to the frame rotating about $z$ at frequency $f$, $\ket{\tilde\psi }=R(t)\ket{\psi }$ with $R(t)=e^{i 2\pi ftI_z}$, gives
\begin{equation}
    i \frac{d \ket{\tilde\psi }}{d t}=2\pi \tilde{\mathcal{H}}\ket{\psi },
\end{equation}
with $\tilde{\mathcal{H}}=R HR^\dagger-f I_z$. Using $RI_xr^\dagger=I_x\cos(2\pi ft)-I_y \sin(2\pi ft)$,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathcal{H}}=\left( f_L-f \right)I_z+\gamma _nB_{amp}\left[ \frac{1}{2}I_x \left( 1+\cos(4\pi ft) \right)-\frac{1}{2}I_y\sin(2\pi ft) \right].
\end{equation}
The terms oscillating at $2f=42.48\MHz$ are much faster than the dynamics we resolve ($\sim_{1}\MHz$) and average out, dropping them is the rotating-wave approximation. The constant $\frac{1}{2}I_x$ term is the co-rotating half of the drive, and it is where the factor of $\frac{1}{2}$ from below comes from. The rotating frame Hamiltonian is then
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathcal{H}(c)}=\Delta (c)I_z+\OmR I_x\quad\Delta (c)=f_L(c)-f,\quad\OmR=\frac{\gamma _nB_{amp}}{2}. \label{eq:Hrot}
\end{equation}

For the central column $\Delta =0$, so $\tilde{\mathcal{H}}=\OmR I_x$ and the spin rotates about $x$ at angular rate $2\pi \OmR$. The Rabi frequency and period are then
\begin{equation}
    \OmR=\frac{(42.48\MHz/\text{T})(0.05 \text{mT})}{2}=1.062\MHz,\quad \TR=\frac{1}{\OmR}\approx_0.94\us.
\end{equation}

We can then construct our Hamiltonians. For the central column ($\Delta =0$)
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathcal{H}}(0)=\OmR I_x=\begin{pmatrix}
        0 & 0.531 \\ 0.531 & 0
    \end{pmatrix} \MHz,
\end{equation}
and for the last column ($\Delta =42.48-21.24=21.24\MHz$)
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathcal{H}}(127)=21.24\MHz I_z + 1.062 \MHz I_x=\begin{pmatrix}
        10.62 & 0.531 \\ 0.531&-10.62
    \end{pmatrix}\MHz.
\end{equation}

\section{Question 1.5: Initial State}
All spins are initialised in the down state $\dn=(0,1)^T$. Since this is an eigenstate of $I_z$ with eigenvalue $-\tfrac12$, and does not depend on $B_z(c)$, we expect $\av{I_z}=-\tfrac12$ at every position, which is what Fig. \ref{fig:init} shows. The column-dependent field changes each spin's energy, not its state, so the position dependence only appears once the spins evolve.

\section{Question 1.7: $\pi /2$ Pulse}
On resonance the pulse is a rotation about $x$ by the angle $\theta=2\pi\OmR t$. A $\pi/2$ pulse takes the spin from $\dn$ into the $xy$-plane, so $\theta=\pi/2$ and
\begin{equation}
  t_{\pi/2}=\frac{1}{4\OmR}=\frac{\TR}{4}\approx0.235\us .
\end{equation}
This matches Question 1.6, in Fig. \ref{fig:rabi}(a), $\ev{I_z}$ crosses zero at $0.25\,\TR$. Since the spins start at $t=0$, the state after the pulse is simply
\begin{equation}
  |\tilde\psi(t_{\pi/2})\rangle=U(t_{\pi/2})\dn,
\qquad U(t_{\pi/2})=e^{-i2\pi\tilde{\mathcal{H}} t_{\pi/2}},
\end{equation}
and no new time evolution is needed. The result is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:pulse}. In the central column $\av{I_y}=+\tfrac12$ and $\av{I_x}=\av{I_z}=0$, i.e.\ the spin lies along $y$. The neighbouring columns show ringing from their different detunings, and the far columns stay essentially in $\dn$.

\section{Question 1.8: Free Precession}
We now switch the oscillating field off, so each spin evolves under the laboratory-frame Hamiltonian of Question 1.3, $\mathcal{H}(c)=\gamma_n B_z(c)I_z$, starting from the states of Question 1.7. We simulate $2\TL$ with $\TL=1/(21.24\MHz)\approx47.1\ns$ (the Larmor period of the central spin), in steps of $\TL/100$. The results are in Fig. \ref{fig:larmor}.
 
Each spin precesses about $z$ at its own Larmor frequency $f_L(c)=\gamma_n B_z(c)$: $21.24$, $29.60$ and $42.48\MHz$ for the central column, the column offset by 10, and the last column. This gives $2$, $2.8$ and $4$ cycles in the window. The central column was tipped fully into the $xy$-plane, so $\ev{I_x}$ and $\ev{I_y}$ oscillate with amplitude $\tfrac12$ as a quadrature pair while $\ev{I_z}=0$. The other two columns were hardly tipped by the pulse, so $\ev{I_z}\approx-\tfrac12$ and their transverse component is small and nearly invisible at this scale. Every spin precesses; what falls with distance from resonance is the transverse amplitude left by the $\pi/2$ pulse. Because the precession frequency depends on column, the signal of a spin encodes its position, which the readout in Questions 1.9 and 1.10 uses.

\section{Question 1.9: Aggregated Signal}
Each pixel holds many nuclei, so the detector signal of row $r$ is the sum over columns of the number of spins $N(r,c)$ in each pixel times that pixel's $\av{I_x}$,
\begin{equation}
  P(r,t)=\sum_c N(r,c)\,\av{I_x}_c(t).
\end{equation}
Fig. \ref{fig:signal} shows $P(t)$ for the central row ($r=127$). The signal is dominated by the columns near resonance, which were tipped into the $xy$-plane. It is not a clean sinusoid, because neighbouring columns precess at different frequencies and dephase, and the weights $N(r,c)$ differ between pixels. A single readout cannot resolve individual columns, since the detector only measures the sum.
 
The maximum of $P(t)$ occurs at $t_{\max}=0.65\,\TL\approx30.6\ns$, which we use as the readout time in Question 1.10.

\section{Question 1.10: Reconstruction}
The field profile is shifted by changing the parameter $\mu $ in $G(c)$, which sets the column at which $B_z=0.5 \text{T}$, i.e. the column resonant with the fixed drive frequency $f=21.24\MHz$. For each $\mu $ (each column of the image)
, we recompute $B_z(c)$ and $\tilde{\mathcal{H}(c)}$ for all columns since the detunings $\Delta (c)$ change with $\mu $, apply the $\pi /2$ pulse in the rotating frame starting from $\dn$, precess in the laboratory frame for $t_{\max}$ (the same value works for every $\mu $ because the resonant column always has the same Larmor frequency), and compute $\av{I_x}_c$ for each column and form the weighted sum over each row
\begin{equation}
    I_{\text{rec}}(r,\mu )=\sum_{c}N(r,c)\av{I_x}_c(t_{\max},\mu ),
\end{equation}
which gives one column of the reconstructed image. The result is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:recon}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/reconstruction.png}
    \caption{Original image (left) and NMR reconstruction (right).}
    \label{fig:recon}
\end{figure}

The reconstruction is recognisable as the original image, so scanning the field centre does recover the image, and it makes sense physically. The position is encoded in $B_z(c)$, only spins near the resonance condition respond strongly to the pulse, and moving the resonance across the image reads it out column by column. It differs from the original in three ways.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Horizontal blurring.} The excitation is not perfectly selective.
        Columns within a few pixels of $\mu$ are also partly tipped
        (Table~\ref{tab:rabi}), so they contribute to the signal of the column
        being read. The width of the resonance, set by $\OmR$ relative to how
        quickly $B_z$ changes, limits the horizontal resolution.
  \item \textbf{Ringing and sign changes.} Neighbouring columns precess at
        different frequencies, so by $t_{\max}$ they have accumulated phase
        differences of order a radian or more (about $1.6$\,rad for a column
        detuned by $8.4\MHz$). Their contributions have different signs and
        partly cancel, which produces ringing near sharp vertical edges.
  \item \textbf{Different intensity scale.} $\av{I_x}\le\tfrac12$, and partially
        tipped or out-of-phase neighbours reduce it further, so the absolute
        scale is not the original's.
\end{itemize}
The image is not blurred vertically because $B_z$ does not depend on the row, and so every row sees the same fields and is read out independently.

\section{Conclusion}
We modelled the nuclei as non-interacting spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ particles in a column-dependent static field and a uniform oscillating drive. In the rotating frame the drive gives a Rabi frequency $\OmR=\gamma _nB_{amp}/2=1.09\MHz$, and only columns close to resonance are driven strongly. A $\pi /2$ pulse followed by free precession encodes each column in its Larmor frequency, and scanning the field centre across the image reconstructs it, up to a horizontal blur and ringing set by the finite width of the resonance.
